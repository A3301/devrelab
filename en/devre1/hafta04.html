Background · The Gap Between the Ideal and the Real Op-Amp

“It is not the deviation itself but its effect on the circuit that decides.” This week we measure four deviations one by one and weigh their effects.

1 · Why does it matter?

So far we have assumed A = ∞, Ri = ∞, Ro = 0 for the op-amp. This assumption works to one part in a thousand at DC and low frequency. But if the treble is cut in an audio amplifier, if a sensor amplifier shows 50 mV at its output with nothing at its input, or if an oscillator’s sine turns into a triangle, the cause is the real limitations we measure this week.

2 · Four limitations, four different symptoms

Finite A: the gain comes out slightly low; the error is about (1+Rf/Rs)/A. The larger the gain you ask for, the larger the error — A is a shared budget.
GBW: A falls with frequency. In closed loop, gain × f3dB is constant; every factor of gain costs the same factor of bandwidth. It is a small-signal limit, independent of amplitude.
SR: the output can change at most at 0.5 V/µs. The steepest point of a sine is its zero crossing (slope ωVm). It is a large-signal limit: as the amplitude grows, the limiting frequency falls and the sine becomes a triangle.
Vos, IB: these are DC errors. The waveform is not distorted; the zero point shifts.

Predict first

Is it possible to get a gain of 1000 and a bandwidth of 100 kHz with a single op-amp with GBW = 1 MHz and SR = 0.5 V/µs? What if the supply is increased? What if Rf is made larger? You will find the answer yourself in Experiment 2.

⚠ Points to watch in a real lab

Wire first, then power up; keep the current limit low. Set the current limit of the dual supply to 50–100 mA. Switch the supply off while changing the circuit. Never exceed ±18 V; swapping pins 7 ↔ 4 destroys the IC within seconds.
Oscilloscope ground clips to a single point. The probe ground clips and the function generator ground connect to the common ground of the circuit. Attaching a clip to any other node shorts that node to ground through mains earth.
Probe ×10 setting and compensation. When measuring above 100 kHz, a ×1 probe loads the circuit with its own capacitance (≈100 pF) and you measure a false bandwidth limit. Use ×10 and compensate the probe with the calibration square wave.
The multimeter AC function has limited bandwidth. Cheap DMMs read AC correctly only between 50 Hz and 1 kHz. For a frequency sweep use an AC millivoltmeter or an oscilloscope, not a DMM; otherwise you will mistake the instrument’s bandwidth for the op-amp’s.
Choose the input amplitude according to the gain. With a gain of 100, a 1 Vpp input drives the output into saturation; do not mistake saturation for a bandwidth or slew-rate limit. Keep the output at a few Vpp during a frequency sweep.
Decoupling and short wires. Put a 100 nF ceramic capacitor to ground at each supply pin, and place the feedback resistor close to the IC. Long wires cause oscillation at high frequency and wrong Bode measurements.
Avoid large resistors. A 1 MΩ Rf with an 80 nA bias current produces an 80 mV error; breadboard surface leakage and moisture from your hands also form parallel paths in the MΩ range. Stay within 1–100 kΩ if possible.
Common mistakes: blaming every distortion on a single limit (fmax = GBW/A is not always right; SR depends on amplitude) · confusing slew-rate distortion with saturation (if the output does not reach ±VCC, it is not saturation) · looking for the −3 dB point at half the peak value (the correct value is 70.7%) · assuming that the total bandwidth of a two-stage design equals the bandwidth of each stage · forgetting to multiply the offset shift by the gain.

Inverting amplifier. With the selector on the op-amp board you can vary A between 10 and 106 (in reality, different op-amp types or a weak transistor stage).

    When A is finite the virtual short is not exact: vn = −vo/A Taking this into account in KCL at the inverting node, the gain is: vo/vg = (−Rf/Rs) / (1 + (1+Rf/Rs)/A)

    Experiment 2 · Frequency Response and Gain–Bandwidth Product

    Non-inverting amplifier (Rs = 1 kΩ, Rf selectable); a second identical stage can be cascaded if desired. Vary the function generator frequency and measure the input and output amplitudes with the AC millivoltmeter.

    Lab sheet

      Notes

      DC gain of the non-inverting amplifier G = 1 + Rf/Rs With a single-pole op-amp the closed-loop response is also single-pole: H(f) ≈ G / (1 + j f/f3dB),  f3dB = GBW/G
      −3 dB point: the frequency at which the output amplitude falls to 0.707 (1/√2) of its low-frequency value. At this point the phase shift is −45°.
      With two identical stages in cascade the gains multiply, but the −3 dB roll-offs of the stages add up: the overall −3 dB point is √(√2 − 1) ≈ 0.64 times the bandwidth of a single stage.
      Keep the output amplitude at a few Vpp during the sweep. If the output is large, the slew rate takes over at high frequency and the curve you measure shows SR, not GBW. If the “SR” light on the millivoltmeter comes on, reduce the amplitude.

      Experiment 3 · Slew Rate and Full-Power Bandwidth

      Non-inverting amplifier, large-amplitude sine. CH1 shows the input, CH2 the output. The distortion analyser measures the harmonic distortion (THD) at the output.

      Lab sheet

        Notes

        The slope of a sine is largest at its zero crossing: vo = Vm sin ωt ⇒ |dvo/dt|max = 2πfVm Condition for undistorted operation 2πfVm ≤ SR ⇒ f ≤ SR/(2πVm)
        SR limit: the waveform is distorted (triangle/trapezoid), THD rises quickly, and the limit depends on amplitude.
        GBW limit: the wave stays sinusoidal, only the amplitude falls and the phase lags; the limit is independent of amplitude.
        Do not confuse slew-rate distortion with saturation: in saturation the peaks are clipped flat and stop at ±14 V; with SR distortion the peaks are pointed and the output never reaches the supply.

        Experiment 4 · Offset Voltage, Bias Current and Compensation Resistor

        The input of the inverting amplifier is grounded (vg = 0). With an ideal op-amp the output would be 0 V. Measure the DC shift at the output with the DMM; Rp is the compensation resistor in series with the non-inverting input.

        Lab sheet

          Notes

          The offset voltage is like a small battery in series with the input; it is amplified by the non-inverting gain: Δvo = (1 + Rf/Rs) Vos
          The bias current IB− of the inverting input flows through Rf: Δvo ≈ IB Rf If Rp = Rs‖Rf is placed at the non-inverting input, both inputs see equal resistance and the remaining error comes only from the difference of the two currents (Ios).
          Measurement method: measure Vos with small resistors (the IB effect is negligible) and the total shift with large resistors; find IB from the difference.
          The DMM’s 10 MΩ input resistance and breadboard leakage are comparable to MΩ resistors. In measurements with large resistors keep the probes still and do not touch the circuit with your hands.
