Background · Two Resistors, One Gain

Last week you saw that the op-amp is useless in open loop. This week we use two resistors to turn it into a stable amplifier with a well-defined gain.

1 · Inverting amplifier: why the minus?

The non-inverting terminal is grounded and there is negative feedback: by the virtual short vn = 0 (virtual ground). A current vg/Rs enters through Rs; no current can enter the op-amp input, so all of it flows through Rf to the output. Since the current flows from the 0 V node towards the output, the output is negative: when the input is at its peak, the output is at its trough (180°).

vo = −(Rf/Rs)·vg,   Rin = Rs

The gain is only the ratio of two resistors: even if A changes tenfold or the temperature drifts, the output stays almost the same. The price: the source sees Rs, so the input resistance of the inverting configuration is limited.

2 · Non-inverting amplifier and follower

The input is connected directly to vp. Rs–Rf divide the output, and the op-amp makes this divided value equal to vg. Since a fraction of the output equals the input, the output must be larger than the input: gain ≥ 1. No current is drawn from the source; ideal for sensitive sensors. With Rf = 0 (or Rs = ∞) you get a voltage follower (buffer).

vo = (1 + Rf/Rs)·vg,   buffer: vo = vg

3 · In real life

Microphone preamplifiers, pH electrodes (internal resistance of hundreds of MΩ!), thermocouple interfaces, ADC drivers, oscilloscope probes… All are built on these two basic configurations. In Experiment 3 you will see that the buffer is not a “useless circuit with gain 1”.

Four-step solution: (1) Does the feedback go to the inverting terminal? (2) Find vp and write vn = vp. (3) KCL at the inverting node (in = 0). (4) Check the result against the supply limit.
Common mistakes: dropping the minus sign of the inverting gain · writing the non-inverting gain as Rf/Rs (forgetting the 1 +) · not checking saturation · thinking “if the gain is 1 the circuit is unnecessary” · forgetting the input-resistance requirement and making Rs small · confusing Vpp with the peak value on the oscilloscope (Vpp = 2·peak).

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

Connect, check, then power. Build the circuit on the breadboard with the supply off. Cut the supply while changing resistors; removing a component from a live circuit momentarily leaves the op-amp in open loop.
Oscilloscope ground clip. The ground clips of both probes are connected to mains earth inside the oscilloscope. Attach the clip only to the common ground of the circuit; if you clip it to the end of Rf or to the output, you short that point to ground.
The function generator’s 50 Ω and the “High-Z” setting. The generator display usually assumes a 50 Ω load; into a high-impedance input you may see twice the amplitude shown on the display. Always measure the input amplitude with the oscilloscope.
Check the resistor colour code and power rating. 8.2 kΩ (grey-red-red) and 82 kΩ (grey-red-orange) differ by only one band. Measure with a multimeter before inserting. In op-amp circuits 1/4 W is enough, but for small load resistors calculate P = v2/R.
Do not short the output; avoid small loads. The 741 output is limited to ≈25 mA; even with a 100 Ω load it chokes at 2.5 V and heats up. Loads such as loudspeakers or relays need a power stage (transistor/buffer).
The multimeter also has an internal resistance. DMM inputs are typically 10 MΩ. When measuring a source with internal resistance of the order of MΩ, the instrument itself pulls the voltage down; when measuring current, use the fused mA input and move the lead back to the V input afterwards.
Decoupling and floating inputs. Put a 100 nF ceramic capacitor on each supply pin. Never leave the input of a non-inverting stage floating: when the source is removed the input bias current has no path and the output drifts into saturation. A 100 kΩ–1 MΩ resistor from the input to ground can be added.

Experiment 1 · Inverting Amplifier: Gain, Phase and Clipping

CH1 shows the generator terminal voltage (vg), CH2 the output (vo). Choose Rs, Rf and RL on the resistor box.

Lab sheet

    Info

    Find the gain from the Vpp ratio: |G| = CH2 Vpp / CH1 Vpp The sign is read from the phase: put one cursor on an input peak and the other on the nearest output trough; Δφ = 360°·f·Δt
    Clipping has two different causes: the supply limit (|vo| → Vsat) or the output current limit (io = vo/RL + vo/Rf → 25 mA). Look at |vn|max on the schematic: when clipping starts, the virtual ground breaks down.
    The generator’s 50 Ω internal resistance is in series with Rs. Since CH1 is connected to the generator terminal, the measured ratio is still Rf/Rs; but if you choose a small Rs, the generator terminal voltage drops.
    In a real circuit do not leave it clipping for long; some op-amps are slow to come out of saturation (phase reversal, “latch-up”). Take the measurement and reduce the amplitude immediately.

      Experiment 3 · Loading Effect and Voltage Follower (Buffer)

      The sensor is a Thévenin source with open-circuit voltage vTh and internal resistance Rint. Connect the load directly or through a µA741 follower.

      Lab sheet

        Info

        Without a buffer the internal resistance and the load form a voltage divider: vL = vTh · RL/(Rint + RL) Rule: the load resistance should be at least 10–100 times the internal resistance of the source.
        The input resistance of the buffer is ≈ A·Ri = 105 × 2 MΩ (practically infinite). No current is drawn from the sensor; the op-amp supplies the load current from the supply. The voltage gain is 1, the power gain is very large.
        The horizontal axis of the plot is the logarithm of RL: 2 → 100 Ω, 4 → 10 kΩ, 6 → 1 MΩ, 7 → DMM only.
        When measuring high-internal-resistance sources (pH electrode, piezo sensor, charged capacitor), even the DMM’s 10 MΩ causes an error. For such measurements use a buffer before the instrument or choose an electrometer-type instrument.

        Experiment 4 · Design Task: Resistor Selection and Saturation Margin

        You have been given a personal design task. Choose the topology, pick Rs and Rf from the E12 drawer and verify on the oscilloscope. The actual resistor values differ from their labels by up to 5 %.

        Task

        Lab sheet

          Info

          Design order: (1) Topology from the phase requirement. (2) Rs from the input-resistance requirement (in the inverting configuration Rin = Rs). (3) Ideal value of Rf from the gain. (4) Nearest E12 value; check the gain error. (5) Saturation margin: |G|·vg,peak ≤ 0.9·Vsat.
          Think of the E12 ratios together: if no single Rf fits, shift Rs by one step as well. For example 4.7 k / 47 k for −10, and 4.7 k / 56 k or 3.3 k / 39 k for −12.
          In a real design do not read the resistor value from its label only: measure it with a multimeter before inserting it. The ratio of two 5 % resistors may deviate by 10 % in the worst case.
