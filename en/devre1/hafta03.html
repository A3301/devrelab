Background · Adding, Subtracting and Cancelling Noise

Two of the “operations” that gave the op-amp its name: addition and subtraction. The second is the heart of industrial measurement.

1 · Summing amplifier: the currents meet at the virtual ground

Connect several branches to the input of an inverting amplifier. Since the node is a virtual ground (0 V), each branch current is simply its own vk/Rk; the inputs do not affect each other. The currents add at the node and flow through Rf.

vo = −(Rf/Ra·va + Rf/Rb·vb + Rf/Rc·vc)

Where? Audio mixers (each channel’s level set by its own resistor), signal mixing, adding an offset, weighted-resistor D/A converters.

2 · Difference amplifier: amplify only the difference

vb reaches the non-inverting input through a divider, va reaches the inverting input through R1. By superposition:

vo = (1 + R2/R1)·R4/(R3+R4)·vb − (R2/R1)·va

If R2/R1 = R4/R3, then vo = (R2/R1)(vb − va). Noise that appears identically on both wires (the common-mode signal) is cancelled, and the small signal between them remains. Thermocouples, bridge circuits (strain gauges), ECG and long-line sensors are all measured on this principle.

3 · CMRR: the measure of rejection

Real resistors are never exactly equal; a small part of the common-mode signal leaks to the output. Common-mode gain Acm = vo/vcm, differential gain Ad = vo/vd. CMRR = |Ad/Acm|, in decibels 20·log10(·). The CMRR of a difference amplifier is usually set not by the op-amp but by resistor matching.

Common mistakes: writing the difference the wrong way round (va − vb) · assuming vn = 0 in a difference amplifier (vn = vp, not zero!) · forgetting the minus sign of the summer · using the simplified formula without checking the ratio condition · forgetting that the common-mode component can also cause saturation (input common-mode range).

⚠ Points to watch in a real lab

An oscilloscope probe is not a differential meter. The ground clips of the two probes are joined inside the oscilloscope and connected to mains earth. Do not attach a clip to va to measure “vb − va”: you would short va to ground. Measure both channels with respect to ground and use the oscilloscope’s MATH (CH1 − CH2) function or a differential probe.
Never connect directly to the mains. Never run the 50 Hz noise experiment with voltage taken from a wall socket; emulate the noise with a signal generator or a low-voltage source with an isolation transformer. 230 V is lethal.
Match by measuring. A good difference amplifier cannot be built with 5% resistors. If those are all you have, measure the resistors on the DMM’s most sensitive range and pair those whose R2/R1 and R4/R3 ratios are closest; or put a trimpot in series with R4 and null the output in common mode.
Several generators = several grounds. The chassis grounds of two function generators are also joined through mains earth; ground loops by themselves produce 50 Hz hum. Join all grounds at a single point (star ground).
Input common-mode range. Even if the difference is zero, the common voltage vp = vn must stay within the range the op-amp allows (741: ≈3 V inside the supply rails). Also, in a summing amplifier DC offsets add up: even if the sine looks small, the output may saturate because of the DC.
Use the meter on the right range. The common-mode residue is in the mV range; use the DMM on autorange and the DC function, and measure the 50 Hz separately on the AC function. When measuring current use the fused mA input, and move the lead back to the V input when you are done.

    Experiment 2 · Difference Amplifier, Ratio Condition and Common-Mode Signal

    va is connected to the inverting branch (R1), vb to the non-inverting branch (R3–R4 divider). The DMMs measure vp, vn, vo and the current in R1.

    Lab sheet

      Notes

      Order of solution: first the divider vp = vb·R4/(R3+R4) then the virtual short vn = vp, then KCL at the inverting node.
      The “COMMON” button ties vb to va: both inputs see the same voltage. If the condition holds, the output must be zero; this is the simplest test of a difference amplifier.
      The input resistances of a difference amplifier are not equal: source va sees R1 (relative to the virtual short vn), source vb sees R3+R4. Sources with internal resistance add in series with these resistors and spoil the ratio condition; if necessary, buffer the inputs (instrumentation amplifier).

      Experiment 3 · Resistor Mismatch and CMRR Measurement

      Nominal R1 = R3 = 20 kΩ, R2 = R4 = 100 kΩ (Ad = 5). Detune R4 by hand, or insert random resistor sets of a given tolerance.

      Lab sheet

        Notes

        Differential mode: va = −vd/2, vb = +vd/2 (common-mode component zero) → Ad = vo/vd.
        Common mode: va = vb = vcm → Acm = vo/vcm.
        “Record” performs the two standard measurements (vd = 1 V and vcm = 2 V) for the current resistor set. Calculate the CMRR yourself from the table: CMRRdB = 20·log10|Ad/Acm|
        Even with matched resistors the output is not exactly zero: the op-amp’s own CMRR (90 dB) leaves a small residue.
        When measuring a high CMRR the residue is in the µV–mV range. On a real bench, ground loops in the test leads and the DMM offset easily corrupt this value; first short the inputs and note the zero reading.

        Experiment 4 · Recovering a Signal from a Noisy Line

        A remote sensor sends a small 500 Hz signal over two wires. Along the line, equal 50 Hz mains noise couples onto both wires. CH1 shows the vb wire (relative to ground), CH2 shows the receiver output.

        Lab sheet

          Notes

          Single-ended measurement: the receiver measures one wire relative to ground and amplifies the noise with the same gain as the signal.
          Differential measurement: the receiver measures the difference between the two wires; the common 50 Hz is cancelled.
          Residual noise: vo,50Hz = Acm·vcm The signal-to-noise ratio depends on the ratio between the differential gain and Acm, i.e. on the CMRR.
          Set the time base to 5 ms/div to see the 50 Hz, and to 0.5 ms/div to examine the 500 Hz signal.
          In industry, sensor cable is chosen as shielded twisted pair; the shield is grounded at one end only. A shield grounded at both ends forms a ground loop and carries exactly the 50 Hz current you are trying to reject.
