Background · When One Switch Is Not Enough, When a Source Pumps Energy, When a Capacitor Enters the Feedback Path

Three topics merge into one idea: x(t) = x(∞) + [x(t0+) − x(∞)] e−(t−t0)/τ. This week we will see t0 shift, τ become negative, and τ replaced by an integration scale.

1 · Sequential switching: resetting the clock

In real circuits a switch changes position not once but many times: a relay coil is first disconnected from the source, then a snubber resistor is switched in; a flash capacitor first discharges through one resistor, then through two. Each switching instant starts a new circuit and hence a new τ. The bridge between intervals is continuity: iL and vC do not jump; the final value of the first interval is the initial condition of the second. Because the clock restarts at t1, the exponential in the second interval is written with (t − t1).

2 · Unbounded response: negative resistance

In a passive circuit RTh > 0: energy turns into heat and the response dies out. A dependent source, however, can pump energy into the circuit. Seen from the terminals, it looks like a negative resistance that pushes current back in the same direction as the voltage; τ = RThC < 0 and the exponential grows. Mathematically it goes to infinity; the real circuit hits the amplifier’s supply (saturation) or a component breaks down. Used in a controlled way, the same mechanism is the operating principle of oscillators.

3 · The integrating amplifier

If we replace Rf in an inverting amplifier with a capacitor, the virtual-ground current is = vg/Rs charges the capacitor; the output accumulates the area of the input. Ramp generators in ADCs, PWM circuits, charge-sensing detector amplifiers and analog computers rely on this circuit. Its weakness: it integrates DC as well. Even a few millivolts of op-amp offset drives the output into saturation over time; this is why a large resistor (Rp) is placed in parallel with the capacitor and the initial voltage is cleared with a reset switch.

Integrator and timing

⚠ Points to watch in a real lab

Interrupting inductor current produces sparks. v = L di/dt: if you open the switch without leaving a path for the current, the inductor voltage shoots up to hundreds of volts (240 V in a 12 V circuit in Example 3.11). Do not open a switch without a snubber resistor or freewheeling diode across the inductor; this is how relay contacts and transistors get damaged.
A charged capacitor stores its energy. A capacitor can remain charged even after the supply is disconnected. Before touching the circuit, discharge it through a suitably rated resistor (e.g. 1 kΩ / 5 W) and verify 0 V with a multimeter. Discharging with a short circuit welds the contacts.
Polarity of electrolytic capacitors. In the integrator the output swings in both directions, so for Cf use a non-polarized (film/ceramic) capacitor. An electrolytic capacitor under reverse voltage heats up, swells and may explode.
Supply unstable circuits with current limiting. A circuit with negative resistance (unbounded response) runs to the highest voltage the supply allows. Keep the supply’s current limit low and choose component voltage ratings (such as “50 V” on a capacitor) above the supply.
Oscilloscope ground clip. The ground clips of both probes must be connected to the same ground point of the circuit. If you attach a ground clip to the op-amp’s virtual ground (inverting input), the feedback is disrupted and the output saturates.
Multimeter current range and fuse. When measuring inductor current, connect the DMM in series and start on the 10 A range. Touching the leads across a voltage source while on a current range blows the fuse. When finished, move the probe back to the V/Ω input.
Resistor power rating. In Example 3.8, 2.5 A dissipates 12.5 W in the 2 Ω resistor at the first instant. A ¼ W resistor cannot be used; compute with P = I²R and choose a power resistor with at least a twofold margin.
Common mistakes: not writing the exponential with (t − t1) instead of t in the second interval; taking τ in the second interval from the old circuit; assuming a calculation error when a negative RTh appears; forgetting the minus sign in the integrator; taking RsCf for a time constant (it is only a scale factor); using the integrator formula for long times without checking saturation.

    Experiment 2 · RC Circuit with a Dependent Source: Unbounded Response

    Example 3.9. The capacitor is precharged to V0; at t = 0, R and the voltage-controlled current source (g·v) are connected. The oscilloscope captures vC(t) in single-shot mode.

    Lab sheet

      Notes

      KCL at the top node: C dv/dt = g v − v/R = (g − 1/R) v Resistance seen from the terminals RTh = 1/(1/R − g) If g > 1/R, RTh is negative.
      When τ = RThC < 0, the voltage multiplies by e ≈ 2.72 every |τ|. The ratio of two cursor points cancels the initial value: |τ| = Δt / ln(vB/vA)
      In practice the dependent source is an amplifier (e.g. an op-amp-based voltage-controlled current source). Its output cannot exceed the compliance voltage (±Vsat) allowed by its supply: the response gets stuck there.
      The capacitor’s voltage rating must exceed ±Vsat. In a circuit running to 120 V, a 63 V capacitor breaks down. When the experiment is finished, remember to discharge the capacitor through a resistor.

      Experiment 3 · Op-Amp Integrating Amplifier

      Example 3.10. The function generator applies vg through Rs to the virtual ground; Cf is in the feedback path. The reset switch SR sets the capacitor to the initial condition vo(0). CH1: vg, CH2: vo.

      Lab sheet

        Notes

        Virtual ground: all of the current is = vg/Rs flows through Cf: dvo/dt = −vg/(RsCf) With a square input the output is a triangle wave made of linear ramps; its peaks are the instants at which the input changes sign.
        The offset voltage Vos acts like a small DC source at the input; the integrator integrates it as well: dvo/dt = Vos/(RsCf) Adding Rp limits the DC gain to −Rp/Rs; the price is that integration is degraded at frequencies below 1/(2πRpCf).
        Cf must be non-polarized (film capacitor). In practice a MOSFET or relay is used as the reset switch; its leakage current is integrated too, so the reset element is chosen with leakage of the order of pA.
        A real op-amp takes a few µs to recover from saturation; remember that in the saturation region the output does not follow the input on the oscilloscope and information is lost.
