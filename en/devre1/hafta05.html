Background · Why Does an Inductor Oppose “Change”?

The inductor is our first element that responds to the change of current rather than its value. Grasping this difference is the foundation of the transient analysis in Section 3.

1 · Intuitive explanation

Current through the winding creates a magnetic field; the total flux linkage is proportional to the current: λ = Li. By Faraday’s law the voltage induced in the winding is the rate of change of flux linkage: v = dλ/dt = L di/dt. If the current is constant the flux is constant and the voltage is zero: at DC an ideal inductor is a short circuit. If you try to change the current suddenly, di/dt becomes very large and so does the voltage: inductor current does not jump.

An inductor stores energy in the magnetic field (w = ½Li²) and returns it; an ideal inductor dissipates no energy. In a real inductor, however, the resistance RL of the copper wire produces heat (P = I²RL).

Where do you meet it? As the energy-transfer element in switching power converters of phone chargers, as a choke in line filters, as the magnet in relays, as the 30 kV spark generator in a car ignition coil, and as the transmitter/receiver coil in wireless charging.

⚠ Precautions in a real laboratory

Do not interrupt the current of an inductive load suddenly. Opening the switch while current flows through a relay, solenoid, motor or large inductor produces a back EMF of hundreds to thousands of volts: arcing/sparks at the contacts, permanent transistor damage, electric shock to your hand. Use a flyback diode or snubber circuit on every inductive load.
Diode orientation is critical. The flyback diode is connected antiparallel to the inductor: cathode to the + supply terminal, anode to the terminal going to the switch. If reversed, it short-circuits the supply when the switch closes; the diode and the supply are damaged.
Inductors heat up. The I²R loss in the winding resistance heats the inductor; a 100 mH/6 Ω inductor dissipates 24 W at 12 V and within minutes reaches a temperature that burns the varnish insulation. Set the current limit and let the inductor cool before touching it.
Multimeter current range and fuse. When measuring current the probes are connected in series and the 10 A socket is used. Connecting a multimeter on its current range in parallel with the supply blows the fuse. Opening an inductive circuit while the DMM is in series also applies the back EMF to the meter.
Oscilloscope grounds are common. The ground clips of both probes are connected to each other and to mains earth inside the instrument. If you attach the clip to the lower end of the inductor to measure its voltage directly, you short out the shunt resistor. Correct method: reference both probes to circuit ground and use MATH (CH2 − CH1), or use a differential probe.
High-voltage probe. For back-EMF measurements a standard ×10 probe (often rated 300–400 V) may be insufficient; use a ×100 high-voltage probe and make the first measurement with the protection element fitted.
Shunt resistor and power rating. The 10 Ω shunt placed in series to measure current dissipates P = I²R: 0.9 W at 0.3 A. A ¼ W resistor will burn; choose a resistor of at least 1–2 W.
Common mistakes: writing v = L·i (correct is v = L di/dt; L·i is flux linkage, unit V·s) · thinking the inductor voltage is nonzero at constant current · getting a negative energy (w = ½Li² is never negative; i = −2 A also stores energy) · ignoring the passive sign convention and taking the voltage sign reversed · forgetting to convert mH to H · thinking a real inductor has “zero resistance” at DC.

Experiment 1 · Triangular Current, Square Voltage: v = L di/dt

Function generator → Rseries → inductor → 10 Ω shunt → ground. When Rseries ≫ ωL the current follows the generator waveform. CH1 shows the shunt voltage (= 10 Ω × i), CH2 the upper terminal of the inductor; the inductor voltage is CH2 − CH1.

Lab sheet

    Notes

    The slope of a triangular current is constant in each half period: di/dt = ± ipp / (T/2) = ± 2 f ipp So the inductor voltage jumps between two constant values: vL,pp = 2 L (2 f ipp) = 4 f L ipp
    The top of the square is not perfectly flat: in a real inductor v = L di/dt + RLi, so a small triangle (RLi) rides on the square. You will measure this resistance in Experiment 3.
    The shunt resistor is the element in the current path closest to ground; this keeps CH1’s ground clip at circuit ground. Attaching the clip to the lower end of the inductor shorts the shunt and your current measurement reads zero.

    Experiment 2 · Current Profile Design: Voltage, Power and Energy

    A programmable current source drives an ideal inductor. Design a current profile by entering breakpoints (t; i); the source tries to follow this profile. The source’s compliance voltage is limited: if the required voltage exceeds this limit, the current cannot follow the profile.

      Notes

      Voltage follows the slope of the current, energy follows its square. When the current is constant v = 0 (current flows, no voltage!). While the magnitude of the current increases p > 0 (the inductor absorbs energy), while it decreases p < 0 (it returns energy).

      A real current source can change current only as fast as its compliance voltage allows (di/dt ≤ Vcomp/L). When you put a steep fall in your profile and the source hits its limit, that is a controlled preview of the back EMF in Experiment 4.

      Experiment 3 · Real Inductor: Winding Resistance, Heating and Quality Factor

      Measure an inductor in three ways: (A) DC resistance with an ohmmeter, (B) steady-state current and heating with a DC supply, (C) impedance and quality factor with a sine wave.

      Lab sheet

        Notes

        Real inductor ≈ ideal L + series RL. In DC steady state di/dt = 0, so only RL sets the current: I = V / RL,  P = I²RL,  w = ½LI² 24 W is a flow (energy turned into heat every second), 0.2 J is a stock (Example 2.7).
        Sine measurement: the current is found from the voltage across the 100 Ω reference resistor, I = VR/100 Ω. The inductor impedance is |Z| = VL/I = √(RL² + (ωL)²) ⇒ ωL = √(|Z|² − RL²),  Q = ωL/RL
        At high frequency the capacitance between turns (Cp, a few tens of pF) appears in parallel with the inductor; above the self-resonant frequency the inductor behaves like a capacitor.
        When measuring a resistance of a few ohms with an ohmmeter, the probe-lead resistance (≈0.2–0.3 Ω) adds error. Short the probes and press REL (zero). Measure while the inductor is cold: copper resistance rises by 0.39% per °C.

        Experiment 4 · Back EMF at Switch Opening and the Flyback Diode

        +VS → inductor → node X → switch → ground. You can choose a protection element in parallel with the inductor. The oscilloscope in single-shot mode captures each change of switch position: CH1 = node X (×100 high-voltage probe), CH2 = inductor current (current probe, 1 V/A).

        Lab sheet

          Notes

          Inductor current does not jump: iL(0+) = iL(0−) When the switch opens the current must find a path. If there is none it only charges the small parasitic capacitance (≈100 pF): the voltage rises to the order of I√(L/C), i.e. kilovolts; the contact gap breaks down (arc) or the transistor goes into avalanche breakdown.
          Flyback diode: the current circulates through the diode and the inductor; node X rises only to VS + 0.7 V. The price: the current decays slowly with τ = L/RL, so the relay releases late. A zener + diode dissipates the energy quickly at a higher but limited voltage (VS + VZ + 0.7).
          Safety: In a real lab never open an unprotected relay or solenoid circuit while holding it with bare hands; the back EMF easily exceeds a few hundred volts. Do this experiment first with the protection element fitted; make the unprotected measurement only with a ×100 probe and an insulated setup, under instructor supervision.
