Background · An Exponential Journey to a New Value

When a source is switched in, the inductor current or capacitor voltage moves exponentially from its old value to its new steady-state value with the same τ. All first-order circuits fit into a single formula.

1 · Steady state + transient

x(t) = xf + [x(0+) − xf] e−(t−t0)/τ
The first term is the steady-state value forced by the source, the second term is the decay of the difference between the initial and final values. However large the difference, 63.2% of the remaining difference closes in each τ: the intuition that “starting further away settles more slowly” is wrong.

2 · Four steps

  1. x(0+): the circuit before switching in steady state (L short, C open); carry it to 0+ by continuity.
  2. x(∞): the circuit after switching in steady state.
  3. τ: in the circuit after switching, with sources deactivated, the RTh seen from the element’s terminals: τ = RThC or L/RTh.
  4. Substitute the three into the formula; find the other quantities (iC = C dv/dt, vL = L di/dt) by differentiation.

3 · In real life

Rounding of square-wave edges on digital lines (rise time, bandwidth), power-supply soft start, LED and relay delay circuits, the 555 timer, motor inrush current, button debounce filters… In all of them the question is the same: “how long until it settles?”

Three quantities, three circuits: x(0+) from the old circuit, x(∞) from the new circuit, τ from the new circuit with sources deactivated. Deactivating sources is only for finding the resistance.
The function generator’s 50 Ω internal resistance is in series with R: τ = (R + 50 Ω)C. At R = 10 kΩ it is negligible; at R = 100 Ω it changes τ by 50%.

⚠ Precautions in a real laboratory

Electrolytic capacitor polarity and square waves. A ±V (symmetric) square wave reverse-biases an electrolytic every half period. If you use an electrolytic, set the generator to 0/+V (offset) mode or use a film capacitor.
Charging a capacitor like a short circuit. An empty capacitor behaves like a short circuit at t = 0+: the initial current can be as large as Vs/R. In Example 3.6 it is 3 A! The series resistor and the supply current limit restrict this pulse; account for the instantaneous power of the series resistor (V2/R).
Relay coil and freewheeling diode. When the transistor driving the timer relay turns off, the coil voltage can break down the transistor. Do not forget to fit a 1N4007 anti-parallel to the relay coil; if the diode is reversed, it shorts the supply the moment the transistor turns on.
Discharge the capacitor before restarting the timer. A voltage remaining on the capacitor means x(0) ≠ 0 and the delay becomes shorter. In real circuits a “reset” transistor/resistor does this job.
Oscilloscope and generator grounds are common. The generator’s black lead and the oscilloscope clip must be connected to the same point. If you attach the clip between R and C to measure the voltage of R directly, you short C; use the difference of two channels (CH1 − CH2).
Supply limits when the inductor current reverses. In Example 3.4 the current goes from −8 A to +12 A; the supply and resistors must be rated for this current and power (24 V × 12 A ≈ 290 W). Scaling down on the bench (larger R, smaller V) is safe.
Common mistakes: writing x(0) instead of (x(0) − xf) as the coefficient of the exponential; reading xf from the circuit before switching; forgetting the source’s internal resistance or a resistor switched in later in τ; writing t instead of (t − t1) in the exponent at a second switching; confusing 63.2% and 36.8%; trying to read the initial value from a discontinuous quantity such as the current or inductor voltage.

Experiment 1 · RC Circuit and Square Wave: When the Frequency Meets τ

The function generator (50 Ω output) drives C through R with a square wave. Every rising edge is a step, every falling edge a natural response. CH1 shows the generator output, CH2 the capacitor voltage; the oscilloscope runs continuously (AUTO) and triggers on the rising edge.

Lab sheet

    Info

    T/2 ≥ 5τ: the capacitor fully charges and discharges in every half period; vC is a rounded copy of the square wave.
    T/2 ≈ τ: the capacitor has no time to charge fully, a “shark fin” shape.
    T/2 ≪ τ: vC is a small triangle wave around the average value: the RC circuit integrates.
    Rise time is the time to go from 10% to 90%: tr = τ(ln 0.9 − ln 0.1)… that is τ ln 9 ≈ 2.2τ. Measure it with the cursors.
    Do not choose an electrolytic capacitor (≥ 1 µF) while the generator is in ±V (symmetric) mode: it is reverse-biased every half period. Switch to 0/+V mode.

        Experiment 4 · Application: Designing an LED Delay / Relay Timer

        When the Start button is pressed, C charges toward Vs through R. A comparator drives the LED or the relay when vC crosses the threshold (Vth = ⅔Vs). Choose R and C to achieve the given delay and verify it with the stopwatch.

        Lab sheet

          Info

          From vC(t) = Vs + (V0 − Vs)e−t/RC = Vth we get td = RC ln[(Vs − V0)/(Vs − Vth)]. If the threshold is a fixed fraction of Vs, the delay’s dependence on Vs is none: the secret of the 555 timer.
          Long delays need a large C → electrolytic → ±20% tolerance. In a real design part of R is made a trimpot and the circuit is calibrated.
          When the transistor driving the relay coil turns off, the coil current looks for a path. You can try operating without the diode here; on a real board it burns the transistor.
