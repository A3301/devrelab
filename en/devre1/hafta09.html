Background · What Happens After the Switch?

When the source is removed, an inductor or capacitor does not “empty” instantly: while the stored energy is turned into heat in a resistor, the current or voltage decays exponentially. The speed of this decay is set by a single number, the time constant τ.

1 · Why a “natural” response?

After the switch opens there is no source in the circuit; the response is set by the circuit’s own elements. The energy is stored as ½LI02 in the inductor or ½CV02 in the capacitor and is turned into heat in the resistor. Since there is no second storage element to return the energy, the response does not oscillate; it falls monotonically to zero.

2 · Continuity: know what cannot jump

Inductor current and capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously, because that would require infinite voltage (v = L di/dt) or infinite current (i = C dv/dt). By contrast, inductor voltage and capacitor current can jump. In Experiment 2 the inductor voltage jumps from 0 to −20 V, while in Experiment 1 the capacitor current rises at once from 0 to its maximum.

3 · Where in real life?

Relay release time, photo flash, a power-supply output capacitor that “still shocks after switch-off”, memory backup capacitors, contact arcing and freewheeling diodes, button debounce filters… All the same equation: x(t) = X0 e−t/τ

Rule for finding τ: I0 (or V0) is taken from the old circuit, τ from the new circuit. The R in τ is the resistance seen after switching from the element’s terminals: the Thevenin resistance (sources deactivated).
Finding a time: v = V0e−t/τ ⇒ t = τ ln(V0/v). Linearization: ln(v/V0) = −t/τ, so the ln plot is a straight line with slope −1/τ.

⚠ Precautions in a real laboratory

A charged capacitor shocks even when the circuit is off. A large capacitor (especially electrolytic, ≥ 100 µF) holds its charge for a long time after the source is switched off. Before touching, discharge it through a 1–10 kΩ resistor of adequate power rating and confirm with a multimeter that the voltage is zero. Shorting the leads with a screwdriver causes sparks and damages the capacitor.
Polarity of an electrolytic capacitor. The long lead / unmarked side is (+), the stripe on the body is (−). An electrolytic connected in reverse or above its voltage rating heats up, bulges and may explode. Its voltage rating should be at least 1.5 times the supply voltage.
Interrupting inductor current is dangerous. If you suddenly open the path of an inductor carrying current, v = L di/dt rises to hundreds or even thousands of volts: arcing at the switch contacts, breakdown of semiconductors, an electric shock in your hand. Always leave the inductor a discharge path (a resistor or an anti-parallel freewheeling diode).
Oscilloscope ground clip to circuit ground. The oscilloscope ground is connected to mains earth. If you attach the clip to another node of the circuit you short that node to ground. To measure the voltage between two nodes, use two channels and take the difference (CH1 − CH2) or use an isolated differential probe.
Instruments load the circuit. A ×10 probe has 10 MΩ and the multimeter 10 MΩ input resistance. These are in parallel with the discharge resistor: with R = 1 MΩ each shortens τ by 9%, both together by 17%. With a ×1 probe (1 MΩ) the error is much larger.
Resistor power rating and current limit. In Example 3.1 the 10 Ω resistor initially dissipates 2 A × 20 V = 40 W (briefly). A ¼ W bench resistor cannot handle this; use a power resistor as the snubber resistor. Set the supply current limit slightly above the circuit’s expected current.
Multimeter current range and fuse. When measuring current the probe is in the “A/mA” socket and the multimeter is connected in series. Trying to measure voltage in this position blows the fuse. Measure transient currents not with a multimeter but on the oscilloscope with a current probe or a small shunt resistor.
Common mistakes: using the resistance of the circuit before switching in τ (τ = L/R1); writing τ = C/R for RC (a unit check catches this at once); taking parallel resistors as series or vice versa; reading the 63.2% point instead of 36.8%; reading the initial value not from the quantity “after 0” but from the jumping one; thinking inductor voltage is also continuous; computing remaining energy with e−1 (correct is e−2).

Experiment 1 · RC Discharge: You Toggle the Switch, the Oscilloscope Captures

In position A the capacitor charges from the source Vg through R1. The moment you switch to position B (t = 0) the source is disconnected and the capacitor discharges through R only (lecture notes Example 3.2).

Lab sheet

    Info

    Single-shot capture: the SINGLE button arms the oscilloscope (yellow ARMED). It triggers when the switch is toggled (green TRIG'D); the first division of the screen shows what happened before the trigger (pre-trigger). After that you can change TIME/div and V/div to inspect the record closely.
    36.8% method: put cursor A at t = 0 and cursor B at the point where vB/vA = 0.368: Δt = τ. Tangent method: the TANGENT button extends the slope at t = 0; the moment it crosses the zero line is τ.
    CH2 is a current probe (1 V/mA): it shows the current through R. Note that at t = 0 the current jumps while the voltage does not.
    If you toggle the switch before charging is complete (≈5R1C), V0 will be less than the source voltage. This is not an error, it is still an exponential discharge; but read V0 from the screen, not from the source.
    The C value on the box is nominal; film capacitors have ±5% and electrolytics ±20% tolerance. It is realistic for your measured τ to differ from the calculated one by a few percent.

    Experiment 2 · RL Natural Response: Inductor Current Continues, Voltage Jumps

    With the switch closed for a long time, the inductor is a short circuit and carries all the current. At t = 0 the switch opens; the inductor current continues only through R (lecture notes Example 3.1). CH1 shows the node voltage (voltage of R and of the inductor branch), CH2 the inductor current.

    Lab sheet

      Info

      At the moment the switch opens iL(0+) = I0, and this current flows through R in the reverse direction: the node voltage jumps from +0 V to −I0R. As R increases τ = L/R decreases (faster decay) but the voltage jump grows.
      A real inductor’s winding wire has resistance (rL). Since it is in series with R in the decay loop, τ = L/(R + rL). Untick the “Real inductor” box to compare with an ideal inductor.
      Freewheeling diode: connected anti-parallel to the inductor; normally reverse-biased, it conducts when the switch opens and clamps the inductor voltage at ≈ −0.7 V. Because the voltage stays small, the decay slows down: the relay release time gets longer.
      If you set R to “open” and also remove the diode, the inductor current has no path. In reality a kilovolt arc forms at the switch contacts. You can safely try it here; on a real bench never.

      Experiment 3 · τ Design, the 5τ Rule and Measuring τ with an ln Plot

      Choose R and C to achieve the given target time constant, capture the discharge and record many points on the curve. The ln(v/V0) – t plot will become a straight line; you will compute τ from its slope.

      Lab sheet

        Info

        Measuring τ from a single point is sensitive to noise and cursor position. Fitting a line to many points (least squares) averages out the error. Since ln(v/V0) = −t/τ, the slope is m = −1/τ.
        5τ rule: after 5τ, 0.7% of the initial value remains; in engineering the response is considered “finished”. Choose the record window (10 × TIME/div) to be at least 5τ.
        Points in the tail of the curve (v < 3% V0) amplify noise on the ln plot: ln inflates the relative error of small numbers. Prefer points between 0 and 3τ for the line fit; choose a suitable V/div when measuring.

        Experiment 4 · τ = L/RTh in a Multi-Resistor Circuit

        Lecture notes Example 3.7: 24 V source, 4 Ω series, 12 Ω parallel, 6 Ω and 180 mH in the inductor branch. The switch opens at t = 0. Which resistance is in τ? First “look” from the inductor terminals with the ohmmeter, then measure.

        Lab sheet

          Info

          How to find RTh? 1) Remove the inductor, leaving two terminals. 2) Deactivate the sources (voltage source → short circuit). 3) Find the resistance seen from the terminals. Here, since the source is disconnected together with the switch, the 4 Ω is not in the t > 0 circuit.
          For t < 0 the inductor is a short circuit: I0 comes from a current divider. For t > 0, 6 Ω and 12 Ω are in series. “I0 from the old circuit, τ from the new circuit.”
          Do not connect an ohmmeter to an energized circuit: the ohmmeter injects its own small current, an external voltage corrupts the measurement and stresses the meter. First disconnect the source, discharge the capacitors and wait for the inductor current to decay.
