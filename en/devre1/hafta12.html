Background · Two Energy Stores, One Resistor

As the inductor discharges it charges the capacitor, then the reverse happens; on each round the resistor turns part of the energy into heat. A single comparison determines the shape of the response: α versus ω0.

Why it matters and where it appears

Most real systems are second order: the ringing at the output of a switching power supply, the tuning circuit of a radio receiver, the settling of a door closer or a meter needle, a camera image stabilizer… Overshoot, oscillation and settling time arise here. The designer often chooses L and C for other reasons (frequency, size, cost); R is the lever that sets the damping.

d²v/dt² + (1/RC) dv/dt + v/(LC) = 0  ⇒  s² + s/RC + 1/LC = 0

Three cases

α > ω0 (overdamped): two real roots; no oscillation, crosses the axis at most once; the slow root leaves a long tail.
α = ω0 (critical): repeated root; the fastest return without overshoot; v = (D1t + D2)e−αt.
α < ω0 (underdamped): complex roots −α ± jωd; a sinusoid with envelope e−αt.

Formula card (parallel RLC)

Neper frequencyα = 1/(2RC)
Resonant frequencyω0 = 1/√(LC)
Rootss1,2 = −α ± √(α² − ω0²)
Damped frequencyωd = √(ω0² − α²), Td = 2π/ωd
Initial derivativedv/dt(0+) = −(V0/R + I0)/C
Overdampedv = A1es1t + A2es2t
Criticalv = (D1t + D2)e−αt, D2 = V0, D1 = dv/dt(0+) + αV0
Underdampedv = e−αt(B1cos ωdt + B2sin ωdt)
Critical damping resistanceR = ½√(L/C)
Logarithmic decrementδ = ln(vk/vk+1) = αTd
With winding resistance rLα ≈ 1/(2RC) + rL/(2L), ω0² = (1 + rL/R)/(LC)
Do not confuse with the series case: in series α = R/(2L) and the damping increases as R grows; in parallel the damping decreases as R grows.

⚠ Points to watch in a real lab

Interrupting inductor current produces sparks. When the circuit that sets I0 disconnects, the inductor current must have a path (the parallel C and R). With large inductors (8 H!), setting R to ∞ and interrupting the current produces hundreds of volts; the switch and the meter are damaged.
Discharge the capacitor. In an underdamped circuit the voltage rises to tens of volts even when there is no initial voltage (74 V in Example 4.3). Before touching the circuit, discharge the capacitor through a resistor and verify with a DMM. The capacitor’s voltage rating must exceed this peak.
Do not use electrolytic capacitors. Because the oscillation voltage goes in both directions, a polarized capacitor sees reverse voltage. Choose a film or ceramic capacitor; the leakage current of an electrolytic also behaves like an extra parallel resistor and spoils the damping.
The oscilloscope probe loads the circuit. A 1× probe adds 1 MΩ and ~100 pF: in a parallel RLC with R = 100 kΩ this means 10% extra damping, and a frequency shift with small C values. Use a 10× probe; connect the ground clip to the circuit ground.
Inductor measurement and winding resistance. Measure the winding resistance on the DMM Ω range with the circuit de-energized. Touching a live circuit on the Ω range damages the meter. If you choose a cored inductor, L may vary with current (saturation).
Resistor power rating. In a circuit with small R (overdamped) the initial power is V0²/R; for 12 V and 200 Ω this is 0.72 W: a ¼ W resistor heats up quickly. With repeated switching, compute the average power.
Common mistakes: writing α = R/(2L) for the parallel circuit (the series formula); using ω0 in the sinusoid of the underdamped case (the correct one is ωd); writing a single exponential in the critical case; trying to find the second initial condition (dv/dt) from V0 instead of from the capacitor current; mixing up the direction (sign) of I0.

    Experiment 2 · Measuring ωd and α in the Underdamped Response

    The time between two successive positive peaks gives Td, and their ratio gives δ = αTd. First verify the method with the known C = 0.125 µF, then measure the unknown Cx.

    Lab sheet

      Notes

      The peak values lie very close to the envelope (∝ e−αt) and are exactly one Td apart: ln(vA/vB) = α Td The unknown amplitude cancels in the ratio.
      From the measured α and ωd: ω0² = ωd² + α², C = 1/(ω0²L) If R is known, cross-check with C = 1/(2Rα).
      When placing the cursors on the peaks, set the time base to the largest scale at which both peaks fit on the screen; reading errors grow for a waveform that covers only a small part of the screen. On a real oscilloscope remember to use a 10× probe.

      Experiment 3 · Critical Damping Design: Fastest Return Without Oscillation

      Choose L and C, and design R yourself. The oscilloscope automatically measures the 2% settling time (ts) and the zero crossings. Obtain the ts–R curve yourself.

      Lab sheet

        Notes

        Critical damping condition α = ω0: Rk = ½ √(L/C) R > Rk underdamped (overshoot), R < Rk overdamped (long tail).
        In the overdamped case the slow root s1 = −α + √(α² − ω0²) approaches zero: the tail lengthens. In the underdamped case overshoot delays settling. The critical point is the fastest response without oscillation; if slight overshoot is allowed, a slightly underdamped design can settle even faster.
        Critical damping is fragile: capacitor tolerances are typically ±5–10%. In practice choosing R slightly smaller (slightly overdamped) guarantees no overshoot in production. You also have to choose R from the standard E24 series.

        Experiment 4 · Real Inductor: Effect of the Winding Resistance

        An 8 H inductor has thousands of turns; the wire resistance rL is a resistance in series with the inductor and adds extra damping. First measure rL, then measure the damping and compare it with the model.

        Lab sheet

          Notes

          Equations: C dv/dt = −v/R − iL,  L diL/dt = v − rLiL Characteristic equation: s² + (1/RC + rL/L)s + (1 + rL/R)/(LC) = 0.
          In the ideal model α = 0 when R = ∞ and the oscillation never dies out. In reality a damping of α = rL/(2L) remains: the quality factor Q = ω0L/rL is finite.
          With R = ∞ (resistor removed), disconnecting the loader that sets the initial current transfers the inductor current only into C: the voltage may rise to hundreds of volts. Check the capacitor’s voltage rating and insulation; discharge the capacitor after the measurement.
