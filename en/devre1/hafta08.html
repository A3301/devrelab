Exam Preparation Summary · Sections 1 and 2 on One Page

Final review before the midterm: the main idea of both sections, all formulas on one card and the 10 most common mistakes.

1 · The essence of Section 1: two constraints solve everything

An op-amp amplifies the difference of its two inputs with a very large gain. With negative feedback the output automatically finds the value that drives the difference towards zero: vp = vn and ip = in = 0. All amplifier solutions follow the same recipe: (1) write the virtual short, (2) write KCL at the inverting terminal, (3) solve for vo, (4) compare the result with ±VCC.

A real op-amp departs from the ideal in three places: saturation (the output cannot exceed the supply), bandwidth (gain × bandwidth = GBW is constant) and slew rate (SR limit for large signals).

2 · The essence of Section 2: the element responds to the slope, not the value

An inductor produces a voltage opposing a change of current (v = L di/dt): its current cannot change instantaneously, and it is a short circuit at DC. A capacitor draws a current in response to a change of voltage (i = C dv/dt): its voltage cannot change instantaneously, and it is an open circuit at DC. Both store energy and give it back; ideally their average power is zero. So if you see triangular current → square voltage on the oscilloscope there is an inductor, and if you see triangular voltage → square current there is a capacitor.

The 10 most common mistakes

  1. Forgetting saturation: writing vo = 24 V. Compare every result with ±VCC.
  2. Dropping the minus sign of the inverting gain.
  3. Taking the non-inverting gain as Rf/Rs; the correct one is 1 + Rf/Rs.
  4. Treating virtual ground as real ground and letting current flow into it; in = 0.
  5. Using vp = vn with positive feedback as well.
  6. In a difference amplifier, writing without matching the resistor ratios vo = (R2/R1)(vb−va).
  7. Confusing GBW and SR: GBW limits small signals, SR limits large signals; the smaller limit applies.
  8. Thinking that the voltage of an inductor and the current of a capacitor cannot change instantaneously (the opposite is true).
  9. Thinking the series/parallel rules are the same as for resistors: for capacitors the rules are reversed.
  10. Unit conversions: mH, µF, nF, cm² → m² (×10⁻⁴), kHz and rad/s (ω = 2πf).

⚠ Points to watch in a real laboratory

When troubleshooting, measure the supply first. Keep the black lead of the multimeter on common ground (COM); with the red lead first measure pins 7 and 4 of the IC. If the “CC” lamp on the supply is lit, something is short-circuited: switch the output off.
Polarity of an electrolytic capacitor. Long lead / side without the stripe is (+). An electrolytic inserted backwards draws leakage current, heats up, swells and may explode. Do not touch a hot capacitor; cut the supply and let it cool.
Resistance is not measured in a powered circuit. The ohm range injects its own current; in a powered circuit it reads wrongly and stresses the instrument. To measure resistance cut the supply and, if possible, disconnect one lead of the component from the circuit.
The current range of a multimeter is a short circuit. Trying to measure voltage with the leads in the A/mA socket blows the fuse. Check the range and the socket before every measurement.
Do not interrupt an inductor current suddenly. L di/dt becomes large: a spark and a back EMF of hundreds of volts appear at the switch. Use a freewheeling diode in inductive circuits.
Discharge a charged capacitor. Large capacitors hold energy even after the supply is cut. Discharge them through a resistor (e.g. 1 kΩ, 1 W) before touching; do not short them with a screwdriver.
Oscilloscope ground clip. The ground clips of both probes must be connected to the same node; when measuring the shunt resistor attach the ground clip to the ground side of the shunt, otherwise you short-circuit part of the circuit.
Resistor selection. The E12 series has 10% tolerance; for precise gain use 1% (E96) resistors. In a difference amplifier mismatched ratios carry the common-mode voltage to the output.

    A bridge-type sensor gives a differential voltage of 0 → S mV as the measured quantity changes from 0% → 100%; both of its terminals sit at a 2.5 V common-mode voltage and its internal resistance is Ri. The output must be 0 V at 0% and 10 V at 100%.

      Information

      Chain: two buffers → difference stage (R1…R4) → non-inverting stage (Rs, Rf). If the ratios are equal vo = (R2/R1)(1 + Rf/Rs) vd
      If the buffers are removed, the sensor’s internal resistance Ri is added in series with R1 and R3: the gain changes, the ratio of the two branches is upset and the 2.5 V common-mode voltage leaks to the output.

      The black box contains an R, L or C; sometimes an op-amp integrator/differentiator. Look at the two waveforms on the screen and find the element, its equation and its value.

      Lab sheet

        Information

        On passive-element screens CH1 is the element voltage v(t) and CH2 is the voltage across the series 100 Ω shunt: i(t) = vCH2(t) / 100 Ω
        If one waveform is proportional to the slope of the other, there is a derivative relation: the slope of a triangle is constant → its derivative is a square. For a sine the derivative shifts by 90°; check which one leads.
        On op-amp screens CH1 is the input vg and CH2 is the output vo. Watch the sign because the structure is inverting: if the output is decreasing while the input is positive, it is an integrator.
