Background · A Short Journey into the Op-Amp

Spend 10 minutes before starting the experiments: concepts, a formula card and the pitfalls waiting for you in a real lab.

1 · What is an op-amp, and why “operational”?

An operational amplifier is a five-terminal IC that amplifies the difference between its two inputs with a very large gain (A ≈ 105). Its name comes from analog computers: with a few resistors it performs addition, subtraction, integration and differentiation operations. It is the building block of sensor interfaces, audio circuits, filters, measuring instruments and control circuits.

vo = A (vp − vn),   −VCC ≤ vo ≤ +VCC

2 · Three operating conditions

No feedback: the linear region is only ±VCC/A (≈150 µV) wide; in practice the output is always at ±Vsat → comparator.
Negative feedback: the output finds by itself the value that drives the difference towards zero → vp ≈ vn (virtual short); the resistors set the gain.
Positive feedback: the difference grows and the output latches into saturation → memory / hysteresis.

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

Connect first, then power. Build the circuit with the supply output off, check every connection against the schematic, and only then turn the output on. Switch the supply off while changing the circuit.
Set the current limit low. In op-amp experiments set the supply current limit to 50–100 mA. If you make a mistake, the IC does not burn; the supply switches to “CC” mode: that is your warning light.
Reversed supply and overvoltage kill the IC. Swapping 7 ↔ 4 or exceeding ±18 V causes permanent damage within seconds. If the IC gets hot or you smell burning, cut the supply immediately.
Ground (COM) is the reference of every measurement. The supply COM terminal and the ground clips of the generator and oscilloscope must be connected to the same point. Clipping the oscilloscope ground onto another node shorts that node to ground.
Do not leave inputs floating. A floating op-amp input picks up noise and the output saturates randomly. Tie an unused input to ground through a resistor.
Decoupling capacitor. In a real circuit put a 100 nF ceramic capacitor from each supply pin (7 and 4) to ground; otherwise the circuit may oscillate.
Common mistakes: using the ideal formula without checking saturation (there is no 100 V output!), forgetting the minus sign of the inverting gain, treating the virtual ground as real ground and sending current into it, mixing up µV and mV.

Lab sheet

    Info

    An op-amp has five terminals: two inputs (vn “−”, vp “+”), one output (vo) and two supplies (+VCC, −VCC). The supply terminals are often not drawn in schematics but they are always there: they provide the energy and set the output limits.
    On the breadboard the upper five (a–e) and lower five (f–j) holes of each column are connected together; the central channel separates them. The long horizontal rails are connected end to end. Hovering over a hole highlights the holes connected to it.
    If the supply is reversed or ±18 V is exceeded, the IC is damaged. In a real lab too: connect, check, then turn the output on.

      The function generator is connected to the non-inverting input and an adjustable reference voltage to the inverting input. CH1 shows the input, CH2 the output.

      Lab sheet

        Info

        The output takes only two values: +Vsat if vp > vn, otherwise −Vsat. Bad for amplifying signals, but exactly the behaviour wanted from a comparator.
        The output cannot change infinitely fast: SR = |dvo/dt|max = 0.5 V/µs At high frequency the edges look sloped. This is not saturation; the op-amp simply cannot keep up.
