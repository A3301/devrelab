Background · Capacitor: The Memory of Voltage

Take 10 minutes before starting the experiments: intuition, a formula card and the traps waiting for you in a real lab.

1 · What does a capacitor do?

It consists of two conducting plates and an insulator (dielectric) between them. For every charge entering one plate, an equal charge leaves the other; no charge passes through the dielectric, but in the external circuit current appears to flow. The charge stored on the plates is proportional to the voltage: q = Cv. Since current is the rate of flow of charge,

iC = dq/dt = C dvC/dt

The current is set by the slope of the voltage, not its value. At constant voltage the current is zero: in DC steady state a capacitor behaves like an open circuit. An instantaneous change of voltage would require infinite current; therefore capacitor voltage does not jump. There is an exact duality with the inductor: there, the quantity that could not jump was the current.

2 · Where do we meet it?

Power supply filters (smooth the rectified voltage), decoupling (the 100 nF ceramic next to an IC), camera flash and defibrillator (collect energy slowly, deliver it in milliseconds), touch screens (a finger changes the capacitance), timing circuits (RC, Section 3).

⚠ Precautions in a real laboratory

Electrolytic and tantalum capacitors are polarised. The stripe on the body (“−” marks) indicates the negative terminal; the long leg is positive. In a reversed electrolytic the oxide layer dissolves, leakage current rises, the capacitor heats up, swells and may burst through the safety vent on top. Check the polarity a second time before powering the circuit and wear safety goggles.
Do not exceed the voltage rating. The voltage printed on the body (e.g. 25 V) is the absolute maximum; leave a 20–50% margin. When exceeded, the dielectric breaks down and the capacitor becomes a short circuit. Capacitors in series do not share voltage equally: the smaller capacitance takes the larger voltage.
A charged capacitor is dangerous — even in an unplugged device. A capacitor with open terminals keeps its charge for hours. A 470 µF/400 V capacitor inside a power supply stores ≈25 J. Before touching, discharge it through a resistor of suitable power, then verify 0 V by measuring with a multimeter.
Do not short it with a screwdriver. Discharging without a resistor means a current pulse of hundreds of amperes, sparks, spatter of molten metal and damage to the capacitor’s internal connections. Check both the value and the power/energy rating of the discharge resistor (a 0.25 W resistor cannot handle 25 J).
Do not connect an LCR meter to a charged capacitor. An LCR meter and the capacitance range of a multimeter measure with their own small signal; a charged capacitor destroys the instrument’s input stage. Discharge it first.
The oscilloscope ground is a single point. Put the shunt resistor you use to measure current at the grounded end; if you attach the oscilloscope ground clip to another node in the circuit, you short that node to the function generator’s ground.
Inrush current. When an empty capacitor is connected to a source without resistance, the current is limited only by the cable and source resistance. Charge large capacitors through a pre-charge resistor or an NTC; keep the power supply’s current limit low.
Common mistakes: writing i = C·v (C·v is charge, not current); thinking current also flows at constant voltage; multiplying by 10−2 when converting cm² → m² (correct is 10−4); forgetting the initial voltage in the integral; not noticing that, by the passive sign convention, a negative current discharges the capacitor; mixing up the µF, nF, pF prefixes.

Experiment 1 · i = C dv/dt: Triangular Voltage → Square Current

Function generator → capacitor → shunt resistor Rsh → ground. CH1 shows the generator output (≈ vC), CH2 the shunt voltage (= i·Rsh).

Lab sheet

    Notes

    An oscilloscope cannot measure current; we read the current from the voltage across a small shunt resistor: i = vCH2 / Rsh The shunt is chosen small enough not to disturb the capacitor voltage (Rsh ≪ 1/(ωC)).
    In each half of a triangle wave the slope is constant: |dv/dt| = ΔV/(T/2) = 2Vppf Constant slope → constant current. When the slope changes sign the current changes sign too: a square wave.
    The rounding at the corners of the square current comes from the time constant formed by the generator’s 50 Ω internal resistance and the capacitor (τ = (50 Ω + Rsh)C). You will see this in detail in Section 3.
    Always put the shunt resistor on the ground side. If you place it between the capacitor and the generator and attach the oscilloscope ground to one end of the shunt, you short the capacitor through ground and see a wrong curve.

    Experiment 2 · Design a Voltage Profile: Current, Power and Energy

    The arbitrary waveform generator (AWG) applies the piecewise-linear voltage profile you write to the capacitor through its internal resistance Rs. A current probe and a calculator plot the vC, i, p and w curves.

    Lab sheet

      Notes

      Profile format: t(ms):v(V) pairs separated by commas. Example: 0:0, 2:20, 4:20, 7:-10, 9:-10. If you write two different voltages at the same time (3:0, 3:10) you request a step (jump). Use a decimal point in values.
      When the power p = v i is positive the capacitor absorbs energy, when negative it returns it. The energy w = ½Cv² depends only on the voltage; it does not remember the history of the current. When the voltage passes through zero the energy is zero.
      If an ideal source could change the voltage as a step, we would have i = C·Δv/0 → ∞. In practice the source’s internal resistance limits the current: peak current ≈ Δv/Rs, and the voltage reaches its new value in ≈5RsC.
      Connecting a real source to a large capacitor with a voltage step stresses the source’s current limit, fuses and switch contacts (contacts may weld). In practice a pre-charge resistor or soft start is used.

      Experiment 3 · Capacitor Types and the Real Capacitor Model

      Choose a capacitor from the drawer, measure it with the LCR meter, connect it to the DC supply through a series resistor and examine the leakage current, polarity and voltage limit. In the real model there is a leakage resistance in parallel with C and a series ESR.

      Lab sheet

        Notes

        Real capacitor model: ideal C + series ESR (equivalent series resistance: plate, electrolyte and lead resistances) + parallel Rleak (leakage: the dielectric is not a perfect insulator). ESR and leakage are largest in electrolytics.
        Why polarised? The dielectric of an electrolytic is a very thin oxide grown electrochemically on the aluminium of the positive terminal. A reverse voltage dissolves this oxide: leakage rises rapidly, the electrolyte heats up and produces gas, the body swells and the safety vent opens.
        Once a capacitor has charged at DC, ideally the current falls to zero. The small persistent current read on the ammeter is the leakage current; in an electrolytic it is large at first connection and decreases within a few minutes.
        In a real lab, reverse-polarity or over-voltage tests are not done. We do this experiment only in the virtual environment. Do not fit an electrolytic of unknown polarity into a circuit; replace a swollen capacitor or one with a bulging top immediately.

        Experiment 4 · Safely Discharging a Charged Capacitor

        The filter capacitor in an unplugged device has stayed charged. Choose a suitable discharge resistor and close switch S; watch on the voltmeter as it falls below 50 V (the limit considered safe to touch).

        Lab sheet

          Notes

          Discharge through resistor R: v(t) = V0 e−t/τ,  τ = RC The peak current V0/R and peak power V02/R occur at the instant the switch closes. All of the capacitor energy ½CV02 turns into heat in the resistor.
          Time to reach the safe voltage: ts = RC ln(V0/Vs) After 5τ the voltage falls to 0.7% of its initial value.
          Resistor selection is a trade-off: a small R discharges quickly but the peak current and peak power grow; a large R is safe but slow. The resistor must be able to absorb the energy (J) with its thermal mass; the continuous power rating (W) alone is not a sufficient criterion.
          In practice connect the discharge resistor with an insulated probe (or with crocodile clips attached while no voltage is present), work with one hand and keep the other in your pocket. Measure with a multimeter after discharging; in some large capacitors the voltage returns by a few volts due to dielectric absorption.
