Background · What Does a Second-Order Circuit Do When a Source Is Switched In?

Spend 10 minutes before starting the experiments: intuition, formula card and the pitfalls of a real lab.

1 · Intuition: where does the current go?

Imagine a constant current Is applied at t = 0 to R, L and C in parallel. At the first instant the inductor current cannot change abruptly, nor can the capacitor voltage: all of the current first goes to charging the capacitor. As the voltage rises, the inductor current increases. Finally (t → ∞) the inductor behaves like a short circuit, the voltage falls to zero and the inductor takes all of the source current: iL(∞) = Is.

What happens on the way is set by α and ω0. If R is large (α small), energy swings back and forth between L and C, the current goes beyond Is (overshoots) and settles while oscillating. If R is small, the current rises without overshoot but slowly. This is exactly the same behaviour as the step response of a control system (motor speed control, suspension, voltage regulator): choosing between “fast but with overshoot” and “slow but without overshoot” is the engineer’s job.

⚠ Points to watch in a real lab

Do not interrupt the inductor current. If the path of a current-carrying inductor is opened, v = L di/dt becomes very large: a spark at the switch and a back EMF of hundreds of volts in the circuit. Before turning off the current source make sure there is a parallel path across the inductor (R or a freewheeling diode).
Compliance voltage of the current source. There is no ideal current source. In the parallel circuit the voltage peaks transiently (31.5 V in Example 4.14); if the source’s compliance voltage is lower than this, the source hits its voltage limit and the curve you measure departs from theory.
Discharge the capacitor. The capacitor may stay charged between experiments; before changing components discharge it for a few seconds through a 1 kΩ resistor. If the initial condition is assumed to be “at rest”, this step is part of the measurement.
Do not use an electrolytic capacitor. In a parallel RLC the voltage may change sign (a negative half-wave when underdamped). A polarised electrolytic heats up under reverse voltage and may explode; choose a film or ceramic (non-polarised) capacitor.
Oscilloscope ground clip. The ground clips of both probes must be connected to the same node. When measuring current across a resistor, attaching the ground clip to a “hot” node shorts that node to ground; use a current probe or a differential probe.
Internal resistance and core of the inductor. The winding resistance of a 2.5 H inductor can be hundreds of ohms: it adds extra damping, reduces the overshoot and lowers the final value to Is·R/(R + rL). An iron-core inductor saturates at large current; L decreases and the frequency shifts.
Resistor power rating. In the transient the peak power in the resistor is v2/R. A 16 V peak at 400 Ω is only 0.64 W and short-lived; for continuous square-wave excitation compute the average power and choose a ½ W resistor instead of ¼ W.
Common mistakes: forgetting to subtract Is when finding the coefficients (A1+A2 = I0 − Is, not I0!) · using the series-circuit formula α = R/2L in the parallel circuit · assuming diL/dt(0+) is zero (it is V0/L if V0 ≠ 0) · computing overshoot relative to the starting value instead of the final value · mixing up mA and A, µs and ms.

    Experiment 2 · Measuring Overshoot and Settling Time with the Oscilloscope

    Circuit of Example 4.15. CH1 shows the node voltage, CH2 shows the inductor current via the current probe (100 mV/mA). The step is applied at the 1st division of the screen.

    Lab sheet

      Notes

      Overshoot: %OS = 100·(imax − i(∞))/i(∞). In an underdamped circuit starting at rest the first peak is at ωdt = π: tp = π/ωd, %OS = 100·e−απ/ωd
      Settling time ts: the instant after which the response enters the ±2% band of the final value and never leaves it again. Since the envelope is e−αt, a rough estimate is ts ≈ 4/α
      The time between two successive peaks is the damped period: Td = 2π/ωd. The logarithm of the ratio of successive peaks gives αTd (logarithmic decrement).
      Zero the current probe (DEGAUSS/ZERO) before clamping it and point its arrow in the current reference direction. A reversed probe inverts the curve; you would mistake the overshoot for an “undershoot”.

      Experiment 3 · Design: 5% Overshoot and a Settling-Time Target

      A 100 mH inductor is given for a driver circuit. The inductor current must settle to a 10 mA step with 5 ± 1% overshoot and within ts ≤ 1.2 ms. You choose R and C.

      Lab sheet

        Notes

        The design has two steps: (1) the overshoot depends only on ζ = α/ω0: %OS = 100·exp(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)) (2) the settling time sets α: α ≈ 4/ts Then ω0 = α/ζ, C = 1/(ω0²L), R = 1/(2αC).
        If only the overshoot is required with a given C: R = √(L/C)/(2ζ)
        The winding resistance of a real inductor (here rL = 20 Ω) adds extra damping: the final value drops to IsR/(R + rL); the overshoot measured relative to the final value decreases only very slightly. Always verify the design with the real component.

        Before the switch closes there is a current I0 in the inductor and a voltage V0 on the capacitor. Start with the circuit of Example 4.5; each run stores a trace so you can compare them overlaid.

        Lab sheet

          Notes
