Background · Every Element Is a Complex Number

In the phasor domain, differentiation is multiplication by jω. This single rule gathers the behavior of the three elements into one Ohm’s law: V = Z I.

1 · Three elements, three phases

Resistor: v = Ri ⇒ V = R I. Voltage and current are in phase.
Inductor: v = L di/dt ⇒ V = jωL I. Since j = 1∠90°, the voltage leads the current by 90°. The inductor opposes changes in current: the voltage is largest before the current peaks (while the current changes fastest).
Capacitor: i = C dv/dt ⇒ I = jωC V, V = I/(jωC) = −j I/(ωC). The current leads the voltage by 90°.

ZR = R,  ZL = jωL,  ZC = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC)

Mnemonic “ELI the ICE man”: E-L-I: in an inductor (L) the voltage (E) comes before the current (I); I-C-E: in a capacitor (C) the current (I) comes before the voltage (E).

2 · Variation with frequency

XL = ωL is directly proportional to frequency, |XC| = 1/(ωC) inversely proportional. At DC (ω = 0) the inductor is a short circuit and the capacitor an open circuit; at very high frequency the reverse. Audio filters, power factor correction and radio tuning rely on this difference.

3 · Impedance and admittance

Z = R + jX (resistance + reactance), Y = 1/Z = G + jB (conductance + susceptance). Impedances add in series, admittances add in parallel. X > 0 is inductive, X < 0 capacitive. Note: G = 1/R holds only for a pure resistance; in general G = R/(R² + X²).

Worked mini example (Example 1.16)

vs = 50cos1000t V, series R = 30 Ω, L = 60 mH, C = 50 µF.
1) ZL = j1000·0.06 = j60 Ω; ZC = −j/(1000·50·10−6) = −j20 Ω.
2) Z = 30 + j60 − j20 = 30 + j40 = 50∠53.13° Ω (inductive).
3) I = 50∠0°/50∠53.13° = 1∠−53.13° A ⇒ i = cos(1000t − 53.13°) A.
4) VR = 30∠−53.13°, VL = 60∠36.87°, VC = 20∠−143.13°; their sum is 50∠0° (KVL ✓). Note: |VL| = 60 V is larger than the source!

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

Do not apply AC to a polarized electrolytic capacitor. During the reverse half-cycle the oxide layer breaks down, the capacitor heats up, swells and may explode. In AC experiments use film/ceramic or non-polarized (bipolar) capacitors; make sure the voltage rating is larger than the peak value.
Oscilloscope ground and element voltage. In a series circuit, if you attach the ground clip of a single-ended probe to one end of a middle element to measure its voltage, you short the rest of the circuit. Either use a differential probe or measure with two channels and take the difference with the oscilloscope’s MATH (CH1 − CH2) function.
Current measurement: Current is measured as the voltage across a small shunt resistor (i = vshunt/Rshunt). Place the shunt on the grounded side so that it coincides with the oscilloscope ground. Never connect a multimeter in parallel while it is on a current range; its fuse blows or the meter is damaged.
Unexpectedly high voltage at resonance. In a series LC circuit near resonance, the inductor and capacitor voltages can be much larger than the source voltage; they can exceed it even off resonance (60 V > 50 V in Example 1.16). Choose component voltage ratings generously for this reason.
Discharge charged capacitors. After dismantling the circuit, do not handle large capacitors before discharging them through a resistor (e.g. 1 kΩ, 5 W); discharging with a shorting wire causes sparks and contact damage.
Do not open an inductor circuit while current flows. Suddenly interrupted current produces a high voltage L di/dt. Turn off the source first. In iron-core inductors a large current saturates the core and the value of L drops.
Common mistakes: Taking capacitor reactance as positive (XC = −1/ωC) · forgetting the j in jωL (the inductor then looks like a resistor) · writing 230 V rms as the phasor amplitude (peak is 325.3 V) · using f instead of ω (XL = 2πfL) · adding impedances of parallel elements directly · applying G = 1/R to a series RL · reading the phase difference backwards (θv − θi, not the reverse).

Experiment 1 · Voltage–Current Phase Relation in R, L and C

Generator (50 Ω) → element → 10 Ω shunt → ground. CH1 shows the element voltage via a differential probe, CH2 shows the shunt voltage (i·10 Ω).

Lab sheet

    Information

    Phase difference θ = θv − θi = 360°·Δt/T. Δt is the time from the CH1 (voltage) peak to the CH2 (current) peak: if the CH2 peak comes later, the current lags and θ > 0 (inductive).
    |Z| = Vm/Im; Im = (CH2 peak)/10 Ω. Since the shunt is a pure resistance, it is in phase with the current; it does not disturb the phase measurement.
    Why does the voltage lead in an inductor? v = L di/dt: the voltage is proportional to the slope of the current. When the current crosses zero its slope is largest, so the voltage peaks; the current reaches its peak a quarter period later. In a capacitor the roles swap: i = C dv/dt, the current follows the slope of the voltage and leads.
    Because of the winding resistance (rL) of a real inductor, the measured angle comes out slightly less than 90°. Select “Real” in the model selection to see the difference; state in the report which model you used.

    Experiment 2 · Frequency Sweep: |Z| and θ

    The element is connected to the AC power amplifier; the true-rms voltmeter is across the element, the ammeter is in series, and the phase meter reads θv − θi.

    Lab sheet

      Information

      Both axes of the graph are logarithmic: x = log10f, y = log10|Z|. On this scale XL = 2πfL is a line of slope +1 and |XC| = 1/(2πfC) a line of slope −1; a resistor is a horizontal line.
      To find L: L = |Z|/(2πf). For C: C = 1/(2πf|Z|). Average over several points.
      Why log–log? When we sweep the frequency from 20 Hz to 100 kHz, |Z| varies over five decades. On a linear axis small values stick to zero; on a logarithmic axis each decade has the same length and power laws become straight lines.
      The ammeter also has a frequency range, and with low-impedance elements (a capacitor at high f, an inductor at low f) the current grows. Keep the voltage low so as not to exceed the amplifier’s current limit and the element’s power rating.

        Experiment 4 · Unknown Element and Real Capacitor (ESR)

        Black box X contains a single element (R, L or C). Black box Y is a non-polarized (bipolar) capacitor; you will measure its equivalent series resistance (ESR).

        Lab sheet

          Information

          Identifying the type: if θ ≈ 0° and |Z| is independent of frequency, it is a resistor; if θ ≈ +90° and |Z| increases with f, an inductor; if θ ≈ −90° and |Z| decreases with f, a capacitor. Measuring at a single frequency is not enough; compare at two frequencies.
          Real capacitor model: Z = ESR − j/(ωC). ESR = Re{Z} = |Z| cos θ. At low frequency the reactance dominates (θ ≈ −90°); at high frequency 1/(ωC) becomes small and the ESR comes to the fore, so θ moves toward zero.
          Why does ESR matter? In switch-mode power supplies, high-frequency ripple current flows through the capacitor; the dissipated power is Irms²·ESR. In aging electrolytics the ESR increases, the capacitor heats up and fails: this is the cause of a significant share of devices that end up as electronic waste.
          Do not apply high voltage to an unknown element; start with a low amplitude. If the box turns out to be a capacitor and it is polarized, applying AC is dangerous: in this experiment the boxes are non-polarized, but in practice check the label.
