Background · Familiar Methods, Now with Complex Numbers

The KCL/KVL rules you learned for resistive circuits do not change; only Z replaces R and phasors replace real numbers.

1 · Why nodal and mesh analysis?

Real circuits (the filter stage of a sound card, a power distribution network, the input filter of a motor drive) contain several sources and dozens of elements. Series–parallel reduction soon gets stuck. Nodal and mesh analysis give a systematic recipe that works for every circuit: N−1 equations for N−1 nodes. Circuit simulators (SPICE) do exactly this in AC analysis: they build the admittance matrix and solve the complex linear system.

[Y][V] = [I]   ⇔   [Z][I] = [V]

2 · Step-by-step recipe (nodal)

  1. Choose the reference node (usually the node with the most elements connected).
  2. Convert sources to phasors and elements to Z = R, jωL, 1/(jωC) or Y = 1/Z.
  3. Ykk = sum of all admittances connected to node k.
  4. Ykm = the negative of the admittance between k and m.
  5. Ik = sum of source currents entering the node (voltage source + series Z → Norton: Vs/Z).
  6. Solve (Cramer or Gauss) and check by substituting the result back into one equation.

3 · At a glance: two phasors of different frequencies

Left: two phasors and their sum (black). Right: time waveform of the sum.
At the same frequency the two arrows rotate together and the angle between them stays constant: the sum is also a phasor of constant length. At different frequencies the angle between them opens as (ω2−ω1)t; the length of the sum keeps changing, so it cannot be represented by a single phasor.

Worked mini example (Example 1.30)

Vs = 10∠0° V, 1 Ω in series; node 1–ground 5 Ω; between nodes 1–2 j2 Ω; node 2–ground 10 Ω; Is = 2∠0° A enters node 2.
Node 1: (1 + 0.2 − j0.5)V1 + j0.5V2 = 10  ·  Node 2: j0.5V1 + (0.1 − j0.5)V2 = 2.
Why is the off-diagonal term +j0.5? Because Y12 = −1/(j2) = −(−j0.5) = +j0.5. The sign flipped twice: once from the “neighbour is negative” rule and once from 1/j = −j.
Δ = 0.12 − j0.65 ⇒ V1 = 9.20 − j0.16 V, V2 = 9.59 + j1.92 V. In Experiment 1 you will test these results on the bench.

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

The oscilloscope ground is a single point. The ground clips of both probes are connected to each other and to the mains protective earth inside the oscilloscope. If you attach the CH2 ground to node 1, you short node 1 to ground and the circuit changes. To see the voltage between two nodes (V1−V2) use the math channel (CH1−CH2) or a differential probe.
Measuring mains-powered circuits. The power amplifier in these experiments is isolated from the mains. In a circuit connected directly to the mains, if the oscilloscope ground touches the live conductor a short circuit and an arc occur; such measurements are made only with an isolation transformer or an isolated differential probe. Disconnecting the oscilloscope’s protective earth (“defeating the plug earth”) is never a solution.
Never leave a current source’s output open. When its load opens, a current source raises its voltage to maintain the current (up to the compliance voltage limit). In the experiment, set Is to zero before changing node 2; on an open circuit the source’s “OVL” warning lights up.
The multimeter’s AC range and frequency limit. Cheap multimeters are “average-responding” and show the correct rms only for a pure sine; most are also inaccurate above 400 Hz–1 kHz. A true-rms instrument is required for the two-frequency waveform in Experiment 3. When measuring current, mind the 10 A input and its fuse; move the lead back to the V input before measuring voltage again.
Element power and current ratings. 1 A rms in a 1 Ω resistor means about 1 W: a ¼ W resistor burns. The wire gauge of an inductor also limits the current, and its internal resistance rises as it heats up. Check the power of the element you choose in advance with P = Irms²R.
Electrolytic capacitors are not used in AC. A polarized electrolytic capacitor fails during the reverse half-cycle, swells and may explode. In AC circuits choose film (polypropylene) or non-polarized (bipolar) capacitors; at the end of the experiment discharge large capacitors through a resistor.
Common mistakes: writing 1/(jωL) as +j/(ωL); forgetting to make the off-diagonal term negative; adding mesh currents in the shared branch (take the difference); comparing the voltmeter’s rms value with the peak phasor; adding phasors of different frequencies; adding rms values directly (the squares are added).

Lab sheet

    Info

    From measurement to phasor: amplitude = Vrms·√2; angle from the time difference on the oscilloscope between the reference (CH1 = vs, angle 0°) and the measured channel: φ = −360° · Δt / T Δt is the time from the CH1 peak to the CH2 peak; if CH2 peaks first (Δt < 0) the phasor leads and the angle is positive.
    Why is that? The inductor (j2 Ω) adds a voltage leading the current by 90°; that is why V1 and V2 are no longer in phase. In the DC solution (Example 1.29) both were real numbers with the same sign.
    Superposition (same frequency): If you add as complex numbers the phasors measured with the sources switched on one at a time, you get the phasor measured with both sources on. Adding rms values is wrong; the angles must also be taken into account.
    Do not attach the ground clip of the second oscilloscope probe to node 1: node 1 would be shorted to ground. For V1−V2 use the difference (CH1−CH2 math) position of the “probe point” selector.

      Experiment 3 · Superposition and Two Sources of Different Frequencies

      Example 1.34: series 10 Ω and 10 mH; v1 = 10 cos 1000t V and v2 = 6 cos(ω2t − 30°) V. Switch the sources on one at a time and examine the current with the oscilloscope and a true-rms ammeter.

      Lab sheet

        Info

        A phasor is defined for a single ω. Solve each source separately at its own frequency (Z = R + jωL is different for each ω), then add in the time domain: i(t) = |I1|cos(ω1t+φ1) + |I2|cos(ω2t+φ2)
        rms value: the average of the product of sinusoids of different frequencies is zero (orthogonality). Therefore Irms = √(I1,rms² + I2,rms²) At the same frequency the cross term remains; the phasors are added first.
        A voltage source that is switched off is replaced by a short circuit (a current source by an open circuit). On a real bench you remove the source from the circuit and insert a jumper; the “output off” button also keeps the internal resistance small.
        Do not connect the outputs of two power supplies directly in parallel: between two voltage sources of different frequencies a large current flows, limited only by their internal resistances. When connecting in series, make sure each source has an isolated (floating) output.

        Experiment 4 · Circuit with a Dependent Source and a Non-Symmetric Matrix

        A current source controlled by the node 1 voltage (VCCS, i = g·v1) is connected to node 2. This is exactly the small-signal model of transistor amplifiers.

        Lab sheet

          Info

          The dependent source is first written like an independent source (+gV1 on the I2 side); then, since the controlling variable is an unknown, it is moved to the left-hand side: −(Yb + g)V1 + (Yb + Yc)V2 = 0 Hence Y21 ≠ Y12.
          Why is the symmetry broken? A passive branch couples two nodes mutually and equally. A dependent source is one-way: V1 affects node 2, but V2 does not affect node 1 through this path. This is how an amplifier passes signals from input to output but not back.
          In a real amplifier the gain rises as g grows, but if there is a feedback path (here the 4 Ω branch) the circuit may approach instability: as Δ → 0 the voltages become very large. On the bench this means the output stage goes into saturation and the elements heat up; increase g slowly.
