Week 7 · Power in Sinusoidal Steady State: p(t), P and Q

Lecture notes: Section 2.1 Instantaneous Power and 2.2 Average and Reactive Power (Circuit Theory II, Prof. Dr. Ali AKDAĞLI).

By the end of this lab you will

  1. Multiply v and i with the oscilloscope’s MATH channel to see the instantaneous power p(t) = v·i, and measure that its frequency is 2ω.
  2. See that p(t) in a resistor is never negative, that its average is zero in an inductor and a capacitor, and that energy flows back and forth.

Background · Where Does the Power Go?

In Chapter 1 we used phasors to answer “how much voltage, how much current?”. Now the question changes: how much energy, and how fast, does the circuit use? Heating, light, work on a motor shaft and the electricity bill are all answers to this question.

1 · Instantaneous power: an energy flow that changes every instant

In DC, p = vi is constant. In sinusoidal steady state v and i change every instant, and so does their product. With the passive sign convention (current enters the + terminal), p > 0 means the element is absorbing energy at that instant, and p < 0 means it is returning energy to the source. If current leaves the + terminal, write p = −vi.

v = Vm cos(ωt + θv),  i = Im cos(ωt + θi)

Shifting the time origin to the current peak (the choice made in the lecture slides) and applying the cos α cos β identity gives:

p = P + P cos 2ωt − Q sin 2ωt
P = ½ VmIm cos(θv − θi),  Q = ½ VmIm sin(θv − θi)

Three lessons: (1) p(t) oscillates at twice the frequency of v and i. (2) Its average is P alone: the term with Q averages to zero. (3) The amplitude of the oscillation is √(P² + Q²) = ½VmIm; that is, pmax = P + ½VmIm, pmin = P − ½VmIm.

2 · Intuition: carrying water in buckets

Imagine the source carrying water (energy) to a load bucket by bucket. The resistor drinks every bucket: the energy turns into heat and does not come back (p ≥ 0). The inductor and capacitor take the bucket and pour it back a quarter period later: what they take and give is equal, so the average is zero. A real load does both: part is drunk (P, watts) and part goes back and forth (Q, VAR). The energy that goes back and forth does not turn into heat, but it still loads the cable: it means a larger current for the same P.

Solved mini example (Example 2.2): v = 100 cos(ωt + 15°) V, i = 4 sin(ωt − 15°) A.
① Convert sine to cosine: i = 4 cos(ωt − 105°). ② θv − θi = 15° − (−105°) = 120°. ③ P = ½·100·4·cos120° = −100 W → the box delivers 100 W. ④ Q = 200·sin120° = +173.2 VAR → the box absorbs reactive power. Had you taken −15° without converting the sine, you would get the wrong sign for P (a common mistake).

⚠ Points to watch in a real lab

Do not connect an oscilloscope directly to the mains. The oscilloscope probe ground is tied to protective earth; on 230 V mains, clipping the ground lead to any point other than earth means a short circuit and an arc. For mains measurements use an isolation transformer and a differential probe.
Watch the ground clip if you measure current with a shunt. The ground clips of CH1 and CH2 must go to the same node; a second ground clip on the other end of the shunt shorts the load. That is why this experiment uses an isolated current probe.
Wattmeter: current coil in SERIES, voltage coil in PARALLEL. The current coil has a very small resistance; connected in parallel with the socket it short-circuits the source. Wire it with the power off and choose the current range according to the load current.
Resistor power rating. Applying 10 V rms to a 10 Ω resistor means 10 W. A ½ W carbon resistor burns out within seconds. The load resistor heats up; wait before touching it after the experiment.
Discharge the capacitor; do not interrupt inductor current suddenly. After switching the amplifier off, the peak voltage may remain on the capacitor: discharge it through a resistor. Breaking inductor current suddenly with a switch produces a spark through L di/dt; reduce the amplitude to zero first.
Capacitor AC voltage rating. A polarized electrolytic capacitor is not used with AC loads: it heats up in the reverse half-cycle and may explode. For AC choose a non-polarized film capacitor; the peak voltage (√2·Vrms) must stay below the rated value.
Never bypass the fuse. A 230 V, 16 A fuse allows about 3.7 kW. Plugging several heaters into the same socket blows the fuse; replacing the fuse with a larger one puts the cable at risk of fire.

Common mistakes

  1. Taking the phase of a sine expression without converting it: for i = 4 sin(ωt − 15°), θi = −105°, not −15°.
  2. Using the power factor without its sign: cos θ = cos(−θ); without the word lagging/leading the sign of Q is lost.
  3. Thinking Q is a “loss”: Q does not turn into heat; the energy goes back and forth between the field and the source. Only P turns into heat.
  4. Writing P = VmIm: with peak values P = ½VmImcos θ; with rms values P = VrmsIrmscos θ.
  5. Thinking p(t) has frequency f: instantaneous power oscillates at 2f.
  6. Thinking voltmeter × ammeter = watts: V·I is apparent power (VA); it equals watts only when pf = 1.

Why does it matter? Real life

The electricity meter multiplies P by time and accumulates kWh; the number on the bill is the integral of P. Motors and transformers are inductive because of their windings: they draw Q > 0. Industrial plants supply this Q locally with capacitor banks; in Turkey a reactive energy charge is applied to customers who exceed a given reactive/active ratio. The wattage on a home appliance label is its average power; the fuse, however, looks at the current.

Experiment 1 · Instantaneous Power and Average Value in a Resistive Load

The AC power amplifier feeds the load box. CH1 shows the load voltage, CH2 the load current via the current probe, and MATH their product p(t).

Lab sheet

    Information

    In a resistor v and i are in phase (θ = 0): p = VmIm cos²ωt = P(1 + cos 2ωt) p(t) oscillates between 0 and 2P and is never negative.
    Why? v and i always have the same sign: when v < 0, i is also < 0, so the product is still positive. The resistor consumes energy at every instant; it cannot give it back because it has no mechanism to turn heat into electricity.
    Mini example (Example 1.3): R = 100 Ω heater on 220 V rms, 50 Hz mains: Vm = 311.1 V, P = 220²/100 = 484 W, pmax = 968 W, p(t) oscillates at 100 Hz.
    The load resistor is a 500 W power resistor. If P = V²/R exceeds this rating the resistor overheats: you will get a warning in the experiment too. Applying 30 V to a 1 Ω resistor means 900 W! The 300 W of Example 2.3 applied to an ordinary 1/4 W resistor would burn it instantly.

    Experiment 2 · Instantaneous Power in an Inductor and a Capacitor: Zero Average

    Same bench; the load box holds a pure inductor or a pure capacitor. The “Real inductor” option adds the winding resistance (r).

    Lab sheet

      Information

      Inductor: θ = +90° → p = −Q sin 2ωt,  Q = +½VmIm Capacitor: θ = −90° → same form, Q < 0. In both, P = 0.
      Why? While the current grows, the inductor stores ½Li² of energy in its magnetic field (p > 0); while the current falls, it returns the same energy (p < 0). The capacitor plays the same game with its electric field (½Cv²) and with opposite timing: when the inductor takes, the capacitor gives. This is the secret of compensation.
      Mini example (Example 2.4): L = 50 mH, i = 4 cos 200t A. v = L di/dt = 40 cos(200t + 90°) V, p = −80 sin 400t W, P = 0, Q = 80 VAR. The energy entering the inductor in one positive lobe is ∫80 sin 400t dt = 0.4 J.
      Do not switch the amplifier off suddenly or disconnect the inductor while current flows: the back EMF causes a spark. Discharge the capacitor before removing and touching it.

      Experiment 3 · General Load: Reading P and Q from p(t) and from the Phase Shift

      Series RL, RC and RLC loads. Find P and Q from the average and oscillation amplitude of p(t), find θv − θi from the Δt measured with the cursors, and compare the two methods.

      Lab sheet

        Information

        Recipe for reading from the screen: P = (pmax + pmin)/2 ½VmIm = (pmax − pmin)/2 |Q| = √((½VmIm)² − P²) Sign of Q: + if the current lags (inductive), − if it leads (capacitive).
        Cursor method: Δt from the peak of v to the peak of i; θ = 360° · Δt · f If i peaks after v, the current lags (θ > 0).
        Why two methods? The first relies only on a power measurement, the second only on a time measurement. Their agreement is an experimental verification of the formula (P = ½VmImcos θ); a disagreement reveals your measurement error.
        Mini example (Example 2.3): v = 100 cos 1000t V, R = 6 Ω, L = 8 mH. XL = 8 Ω, Z = 10∠53.13° Ω, I = 10∠−53.13° A. P = 500·cos53.13° = 300 W, Q = 500·sin53.13° = 400 VAR, pf = 0.6 lagging. Check: ½Im²R = 300 W ✓.
        In a series RLC, as you approach resonance the current grows and the inductor and capacitor voltages can be much larger than the source (by a factor Q). Do not exceed the capacitor’s voltage rating; mind the amplifier’s 10 A protection.

        Experiment 4 · Wattmeter Reading versus the V·I Product

        50 Hz mains voltage reaches a socket strip through a variac + isolation transformer. The wattmeter, voltmeter and ammeter are read at the same time; plug home appliances in and out.

        Lab sheet

          Information

          In an electrodynamic wattmeter the moving coil is fed by the voltage and the fixed coil by the current; the inertia of the pointer shows the average of the product: W = (1/T)∫ v i dt = P The voltmeter and ammeter read rms: V·I = S (VA),  pf = P / (V·I)
          Why is P ≠ V·I? V·I assumes that the current is in phase with the voltage. In a motor, part of the current goes back and forth only to build the magnetic field: it heats the cable and stresses the fuse, but contributes nothing to the average power.
          Mini example (Example 2.8): 230 V, 16 A fuse → Plimit = 3680 W. Kettle 2000 W + toaster 1146 W + hair dryer 600 W = 3746 W → I = 16.29 A > 16 A: the fuse blows. Removing any one of them is enough.
          This experiment uses 230 V: change connections only with the power off, use insulated (safety-type) banana leads, and work with one hand. A residual current device (30 mA) must be in the circuit.
