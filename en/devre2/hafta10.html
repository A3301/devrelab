Background · Generating Reactive Power Locally

Why are there capacitors in a factory’s switchboard? This section builds the answer step by step.

1 · The problem: low power factor

An induction motor draws reactive power (Q > 0) for magnetisation. This energy does no work; it goes back and forth between source and motor every half period. But while travelling over the line it carries current: for the same P the current is I = P / (V cos θ), so at cos θ = 0.6 the current is 67% higher than at cos θ = 1. Since line loss is I²R, the loss rises almost 2.8 times; transformers and cables are loaded for nothing.

2 · The solution: put a capacitor next to the load

A capacitor absorbs negative reactive power (that is, it generates reactive power). Connected in parallel next to the motor, it supplies part of the Q the motor needs; the line carries only the difference. The motor itself notices nothing: its voltage is the same, and its P and Q are the same. Only the current flowing through the line changes.

QC = P (tan θ1 − tan θ2),   C = QC / (ω Vrms²)

3 · Overcompensation

If the capacitor is larger than needed, the total Q becomes negative, the pf drops again (this time leading) and the current rises again. At light load the bus voltage rises; the distribution company also charges a penalty for capacitive reactive energy.

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

A capacitor bank is dangerous even after the power is cut. A capacitor of hundreds of µF charged at 400 V stores lethal energy (½CV² ≈ 50 J and more). Discharge resistors must be fitted; wait at least 1 min before touching and verify by measuring the voltage.
Never open the secondary of a current transformer. If the secondary of a CT used for panel metering is left open while carrying load, thousands of volts appear and the insulation breaks down. Before removing the meter, bridge the secondary with a shorting terminal.
Switching a capacitor under voltage. At switch-on a very large charging current pulse occurs; contactor contacts can weld. Special capacitor contactors (with pre-insertion resistors) are used for compensation, and a step must be allowed to discharge before it is switched in again.
Harmonics and resonance. In installations with drive (inverter) loads, the capacitor can resonate with the grid inductance at a harmonic frequency and overheat or explode. In that case detuned (harmonic-filter) reactor compensation is required.
Overcompensation raises the voltage. When the load decreases, capacitors left in circuit raise the bus voltage; sensitive equipment may be damaged. Set the relay to the correct target cos θ and switch steps out together with the load.
Oscilloscope on mains measurements. Use a differential probe for measurements on the 230/400 V side; connecting the ground clip to the phase causes a short circuit. In motor experiments fit protective covers over rotating parts.
Common mistakes: using P instead of QC when computing C (C comes out twice as large, overcompensation) · adding apparent powers arithmetically · using P = V²/R in a series circuit with the total voltage across R and X · ignoring the sign of the source power · forgetting the ½ factor with peak-value phasors · thinking compensation reduces the motor’s P.

Experiment 1 · Motor Load and pf Correction with a Parallel Capacitor

A single-phase source supplies the motor bus through a small line impedance (0.05 + j0.15 Ω). The capacitor bank is in parallel with the motor. The power analyser is on the source side; the voltmeter and pf meter are on the bus.

Lab sheet

    Information

    Capacitor for a target pf: QC = P(tan θ1 − tan θ2),  C = QC/(ωV²) Here V is the voltage of the bus to which the capacitor is connected.
    Since the motor is a fixed impedance, its P and Q vary with the square of the bus voltage; so does the capacitor’s QC. Therefore the bus pf is independent of the voltage.
    Why does the current drop while the motor power stays the same? The voltage at the motor terminals stays almost the same; the motor draws the same P and Q. Most of Q is now generated locally by the capacitor; the line only has to carry P and a small residual Q. I = |Sline|/V decreases, and the line loss falls with I².
    Worked mini example (Example 2.19): 250 V, 60 Hz bus; loads 8 − j6 kVA and 12 + j16 kVA. Total 20 + j10 kVA, pf = 0.894 lagging. For pf = 1, QC = 10 kVAR, C = 10 000/(377 × 250²) = 424.4 µF. If P = 20 kW is used instead of QC, C = 848.8 µF: twice as large, serious overcompensation.
    Switch the capacitor bank in step by step and check the bus voltage after each change. In real banks, once a step has been switched out, it must not be switched in again before the discharge time (typically 60 s) has elapsed.

    Experiment 2 · Line Loss, Voltage Drop and Power Balance

    The source supplies a load through Zline = Rh + jXh. The load can be connected in series (R + jX) or in parallel (R ∥ jX). Connect the power analyser to the source, the line or the load and measure.

    Lab sheet

      Information

      In a series circuit find the total impedance first, then the current: I = Vs/(Zline + ZL) The power of each element is found from S = |I|² Z; the power delivered by the source is Ss = Vs I*
      The power balance holds for P and Q separately: Ps = Pline + PL, Qs = Qline + QL. Efficiency: η = PL/Ps.
      Why is P = |VL|²/R wrong? In a series load |VL| is the total voltage across R and jX, not the voltage across R alone. In Example 2.18 this mistake gives 1408 W; the correct value is |I|²R = 975 W.
      Worked mini example (Example 2.22): 100 V, line 2 + j6 Ω, load 10 Ω ∥ −j5 Ω. Parallel equivalent 2 − j4 Ω; total 4 + j2 Ω; I = 20 − j10 A, |I|² = 500. Sline = 1000 + j3000, Sload = 1000 − j2000, Ss = V I* = 2000 + j1000 VA. The totals match ✓.
      Line loss is heat. In real installations the cable cross-section is chosen according to the current; an overheated cable loses its insulation and creates a fire risk. Ways to reduce line loss: increase the cross-section, raise the voltage and correct the pf.
      Power vectors: Sline + Sload = Ssource

      Experiment 3 · Economics of Compensation: Penalty, Loss and Investment

      You are designing a stepped capacitor bank for the panel of a workshop (average 120 kW, cos θ = 0.72 lagging, 400 hours per month). Prices are representative values; you can change them with the knobs.

      Lab sheet

        Information

        Monthly energies: Eact = P·t,  Eind = Qnet·t (Qnet > 0),  Ecap = |Qnet|·t (Qnet < 0) Limits assumed in this lab: if Eind/Eact > 20% or Ecap/Eact > 15%, all of the reactive energy is billed at the unit price (penalty).
        The in-plant loss (cable + transformer copper loss) is taken as 2% of P at cos θ = 1 and varies with |S|². The investment is amortised over 60 months (5 years).
        Why not aim for “exactly 1”? The load changes during the day. A bank set to pf = 1 immediately enters the capacitive region when the load decreases, and then a capacitive penalty arises. Since the step size is finite, exactly 1 cannot be hit anyway; in practice the target is the range 0.95–0.99 lagging.
        Worked mini example: 50 kW, cos θ = 0.8 lagging (tan θ = 0.75). Limit ratio 0.20 → Qnet ≤ 10 kVAR. QC ≥ 37.5 − 10 = 27.5 kVAR → with 5 kVAR steps, 6 steps (30 kVAR). Capacitive limit: Qnet ≥ −7.5 kVAR → QC ≤ 45 kVAR (at most 9 steps).
        At the end of its life a capacitor bank must be separated as electronic waste; old-type capacitors may contain toxic PCB oil. Lifetime, the harmonic environment and disposal cost are also considered in the design.
