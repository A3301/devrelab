Background · How Do We Draw the Most Power from a Source?

Seen from the load, every linear source is just a VTh and a ZTh. The question: how should we choose the load so that the average power is maximum?

1 · Two competing demands

If the load resistance is very small, the current is large but the load voltage is small; if it is very large, the voltage is large but the current is small. The power is zero at both extremes, with a peak in between. Reactance only reduces the current: the best choice is to cancel the source reactance.

PL = |VTh|² RL / [ (RTh+RL)² + (XTh+XL)² ]

∂P/∂XL = 0 → XL = −XTh; ∂P/∂RL = 0 → RL = RTh. Together:

ZL = ZTh*,   Pmax = |VTh|²/(4RTh)

2 · The price: efficiency

At matching, the same current also flows through RTh and dissipates as much power as the load: η = RL/(RTh+RL) = 50%. This is acceptable in communication and audio circuits (weak signals); never in power systems: a power plant burning half of what it generates inside itself is unacceptable. There RL ≫ RTh is chosen.

Common mistakes

Writing ZL = ZTh: without the conjugate the reactances add (j4 + j4 = j8) and the current drops. Correctly, with ZL = 3 − j4 the total is 6 Ω, purely resistive.
Mixing peak and rms: with Vm = 19.2 V peak and RTh = 5.76 Ω, P = Vm²/(4RTh) = 16 W is wrong; Vm²/(8RTh) = 8 W is correct.
Applying the conjugate to a constrained load: with the angle fixed at −36.87°, 3000 − j4000 (angle −53.13°) cannot be chosen. The correct approach is to match only the magnitude: 4000 − j3000 Ω.
Thinking “maximum power = 100% efficiency”: at matching the power is shared half and half, η = 50%.

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

Do not leave the amplifier output unloaded or short-circuited. Shorting the output of an audio amplifier burns the output transistors; in tube (valve) amplifiers, leaving the load open creates overvoltage in the output transformer.
Power rating of the resistor box. At the matching point the load draws the largest power the source can deliver. Each step of a decade resistor box is typically rated 0.5–1 W; before the experiment, calculate Pmax and check that the box can carry it.
Transformer secondary. Shorting the secondary of a matching transformer draws a very large current from the primary; in current transformers the secondary must never be left open. Switch off the source when changing the transformer ratio.
RF mismatch can burn the transmitter. If an antenna’s impedance does not match the transmitter, power is reflected back (high SWR) and heats the output stage. In RF measurements the load and cables must be matched to 50 Ω.
Do not attempt “maximum power” on the mains side. The Thevenin impedance of the grid is in the milliohm range; a matched load is a short circuit and draws tens of kA. In power systems the load impedance is kept much larger than the source impedance.
Instrument loading. If the source impedance is in the kΩ range, the voltmeter input resistance (10 MΩ) and the ammeter internal resistance affect the results; with a high-impedance source, choose the wattmeter current circuit carefully.

Experiment 1 · Peak Hunting on the PL(RL, XL) Surface

A resistor box and a reactance box are connected in series with the Thevenin source module. Turn the two knobs to make the power shown by the wattmeter as large as possible. Each of your measurements appears as a point on the map.

Lab sheet

    Info

    For fixed RL, P is maximum when XL = −XTh (the second term of the denominator is zero). Therefore, about the line XL = −XTh, the contour lines are symmetric.
    Sweep strategy: first sweep XL to find where the power is maximum, fix it and sweep RL, then go back to XL (coordinate descent). Two rounds are usually enough.
    Why reactance first? As long as XTh + XL ≠ 0, there is a “brake” in the loop that consumes no power but limits the current. The conjugate reactance cancels it by resonance; what remains is only the sharing between RTh and RL.
    Solved mini example (Example 10.9a): 10 V, ZTh = 3000 + j4000 Ω. ZL = 3000 − j4000 Ω; the loop is a pure 6000 Ω resistance; I = 10/6000 = 1.667 mA; P = I²·3000 = 8.33 mW = 10²/(4·3000) ✓.
    When working with the hidden source, do not reduce the load resistance to zero during the sweep: in a real source this is a short circuit and may exceed the source’s current limit. Always leave a small series protection resistance in the resistor box.

    Experiment 2 · Constrained Loads: What If the Conjugate Is Not Possible?

    Not every parameter of a real load can be adjusted. Try three cases: only resistance adjustable, limited R and X, and a load with fixed angle.

    Lab sheet

      Info

      Only RL: with XL = 0, dP/dRL = 0 → RL = √(RTh² + XTh²) = |ZTh|
      Limited R and X: first set XL as close to −XTh as allowed (the nearest permitted value); then RL = √(RTh² + (XTh+XL)²) (clipped to the allowed range).
      Fixed angle: ZL = |ZL|∠θL, only the magnitude adjustable → |ZL| = |ZTh|
      Why are the magnitudes equated when the angle is fixed? Differentiating P with respect to |ZL| gives a generalised form of the rule RL = |ZTh|: when only one degree of freedom remains, the load matches the magnitude of the source impedance. A purely resistive load (θL = 0) is a special case of this.
      Solved mini example (Example 2.23): 100 V, ZTh = 3 + j4 Ω, only RL. RL = 5 Ω; I = 100/|8 + j4| = 11.18 A; P = 11.18² × 5 = 625 W. With conjugate matching it would be 833 W.
      The internal resistance of the inductor in the reactance box (a few Ω in practice) adds to the real part of the load; the measured best RL may come out smaller than theory by that amount. Measure the inductor with an LCR meter before the measurement.

      Experiment 3 · Impedance Matching with a Transformer and Efficiency

      Example 2.25: The Thevenin equivalent of an audio amplifier is 40 V (rms), 800 Ω. The loudspeaker is 8 Ω. We insert a matching transformer (n:1) with adjustable ratio between them.

      Lab sheet

        Info

        In an ideal transformer V1 = nV2, I1 = I2/n; the impedance seen from the primary is Z’ = V1/I1 = n² ZL For matching, n²RL = RTh.
        Efficiency (ideal transformer): η = n²RL/(RTh + n²RL) As n increases η increases, but the power in the load decreases.
        Why a transformer? Connecting the loudspeaker directly means nearly short-circuiting the 800 Ω source with 8 Ω: most of the current turns into heat in the source’s internal resistance. The transformer makes the loudspeaker “look like” 800 Ω to the source; power is reflected, impedance is transformed. A lossless transformer consumes no power itself.
        Solved mini example: RTh = 600 Ω source, 16 Ω loudspeaker: n = √(600/16) = 6.12. With real transformers the nearest standard ratio is chosen; for n = 6, Z’ = 576 Ω and the power loss is as small as 0.04%. The flatness of the peak allows the match to be approximate.
        A real transformer has winding resistances (copper loss) and magnetising current; the efficiency is not 100%. Turn on the “Real transformer” option and see how the winding losses change the best n and the power. At audio frequencies, do not overdrive at low frequency so that the transformer core does not saturate.
