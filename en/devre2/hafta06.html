Background · Two Windings, One Shared Magnetic Field

A transformer carries energy not by wire but by magnetic flux. That is why it changes the voltage level, isolates circuits, and presents the load to the source as “a different impedance”.

1 · Why does it matter, and where is it used?

In Turkey, energy generated at ~11–20 kV in power plants is stepped up to 400 kV for transmission, stepped down to 34.5 kV at distribution centres and to 400/230 V at the neighbourhood transformer. Why? For the same power, if the voltage rises a times the current falls a times, and the line loss RI² falls a² times. In electronics, small transformers block DC, isolate lines and match impedances (microphone, loudspeaker, Ethernet line, antenna balun).

2 · Two models

Linear transformer: R1, R2, L1, L2, M = k√(L1L2). Air-core and communication transformers. Two mesh equations:
Vs = Z11I1 − jωM I2,  0 = −jωM I1 + Z22I2
Ideal transformer: k = 1, L → ∞, lossless. Only the turns ratio remains: V1/N1 = V2/N2, N1I1 = N2I2 (signs according to the dots).

Animation: the v1 and v2 phasors arise from the same flux; if the dot is on the opposite terminal the v2 phasor rotates by 180°. The amplitude ratio is the turns ratio, and the phase difference is either 0° or 180°: an ideal transformer does not shift phase.

3 · The dot convention (in two sentences)

Voltage: if the + terminals of V1 and V2 are both dotted (or both undotted), V1/N1 = +V2/N2; if one is dotted and the other is not, the sign is minus.
Current: if I1 and I2 both enter the dotted terminals (or both leave), N1I1 = −N2I2; if one enters and the other leaves, the sign is plus. This is because the net magnetomotive force in an ideal core is zero.

4 · Why does the reflected reactance have the opposite sign?

Zr ∝ 1/Z22 and 1/(R + jX) = (R − jX)/|Z|². If the secondary is capacitive it appears inductive from the primary, and if inductive it appears capacitive. The real part, however, is always ≥ 0: a passive secondary cannot return power to the source.

Worked mini example (Example 1.38)

Source 220∠0° V rms, Zg = 2 + j1 Ω; ideal transformer 5:1 (dots on top); ZL = 4 + j3 Ω.
a = N2/N1 = 0.2 ⇒ ZIN = ZL/a² = 25(4 + j3) = 100 + j75 Ω. Why did it grow? A step-down transformer makes the load look large to the source.
I1 = 220/(102 + j76) = 1.730∠−36.69° A; I2 = I1/a = 8.65 A; PL = 8.648² · 4 = 299.1 W. Check: |I1|²·102 = 305.1 W = 5.98 W (Zg) + 299.1 W (load) ✓

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

Mains voltage kills. 230 V AC can be lethal with a current of the order of 30 mA through the heart. Connections are made with the power off, only insulated (safety-type, shrouded) banana leads are used, the one-hand rule is applied, and the circuit is checked by the supervisor before power is applied. The residual-current device (30 mA) must be in working order.
Isolation transformer and oscilloscope ground. The oscilloscope probe ground is connected to protective earth. In a circuit connected directly to the mains, attaching the ground clip to any point other than neutral creates a line-to-earth short circuit: the probe burns and an arc forms. Either feed the circuit through an isolation transformer first or use a differential probe. Defeating the oscilloscope’s earth connection is strictly forbidden.
Start the short-circuit test with the variac at zero. Applying full voltage to the primary with the secondary shorted draws 10–20 times the rated current: the fuse blows and the windings heat up. Start the variac at 0 V and increase it slowly up to rated current while watching the ammeter.
In the open-circuit test the high-voltage side is live. Even if the open-circuit test is done from the low-voltage side, rated voltage appears at the high-voltage terminals. Do not touch the open secondary terminals; make sure the instrument’s category (CAT II/III) and voltage rating are suitable.
Never leave a current transformer’s secondary open. In a measuring current transformer (CT) the primary current is set by the load; if the secondary is opened the magnetizing current is not limited, thousands of volts appear on the secondary and the core overheats. A CT secondary is always kept closed with a shorting link or an ammeter. A voltage transformer’s secondary, on the other hand, is never short-circuited.
Inrush current and sparks. At the instant a transformer is energized, the core may saturate because of residual magnetism and a short-lived current several times the rated current is drawn. That is why time-delay (T-type) fuses are chosen. When power is switched off, the sudden interruption of the magnetizing current causes a spark at the switch.
Instrument connections. An ammeter is connected in series and a voltmeter in parallel; a wattmeter’s current coil in series and its voltage coil in parallel. Measuring voltage while on the multimeter’s current input blows the fuse (and the meter). The wattmeter’s current limit must be higher than the rated current.
Common mistakes: writing ωM instead of (ωM)² in reflection; keeping the sign of Z22 (the conjugate is taken); multiplying the impedance by a² (if a = N2/N1 you divide by a²); always writing a plus sign without looking at the dots; adding phasor magnitudes directly (60 + 80 ≠ 100); thinking the current lags when θZ < 0.

Experiment 1 · Linear Transformer and Reflected Impedance

Example 1.35 setup: 300 V rms, ω = 400 rad/s (f = 63.7 Hz), Zs = 500 + j100 Ω. The coupling coefficient can be adjusted with the core gap. Measure the voltage at terminals a–b, the primary current and the phase difference, and find Zr.

Lab sheet

    Info

    From measurement to Zr: Zab = (Vab/I1)∠θ,  θ = 360°·Δt/T Δt: the time from the CH1 (vab) peak to the CH2 (i1) peak (Δt > 0 if the current lags). Then Zr = Zab − (R1 + jωL1) = Zab − (200 + j3600) Ω
    Why is that? The secondary current induces a voltage of −jωM I2 in the primary. Since this voltage is proportional to I1, the primary circuit “feels” it as an extra impedance. The current is coupled twice (primary → secondary → primary), which is why M² appears.
    Check: With the secondary open, I2 = 0 and Zr must be 0: Zab = 200 + j3600 Ω. This is the easiest way to test the accuracy of your measurement setup.
    Switch off the source before short-circuiting the secondary or changing the load. In a linear transformer the short-circuit current is limited because k is small, but in an iron-core power transformer the same action means high current and sparks.

    Experiment 2 · Open-Circuit and Short-Circuit Tests on a Mains Transformer

    230/24 V, 100 VA transformer (each student gets a transformer with a different manufacturing tolerance). Find the turns ratio and the iron loss with the open-circuit test, and the winding loss and equivalent impedance with the short-circuit test. This experiment is performed at mains voltage.

    Lab sheet

      Info

      Open-circuit (no-load) test: secondary open, rated voltage on the primary. The current is only the magnetizing current (2–10% of rated current); winding loss is negligible, so the wattmeter shows the iron (core) loss. V1/V2 ≈ N1/N2.
      Short-circuit test: secondary shorted through an ammeter, a low voltage on the primary just enough to drive rated current (5–10% of rated voltage). Since the core flux is small, iron loss is negligible; the wattmeter shows the copper loss. The ratio Vk/Vn is the “short-circuit voltage” uk.
      Why two tests? Testing a transformer at full load requires large power and a real load. The total power of the two tests is only the losses (a few watts): large power transformers are tested this way in factories too.
      Specific to this experiment: Make the short-circuit connection only with POWER OFF. Do not apply power without first setting the variac to zero. The rated current is 0.435 A; if the primary current exceeds 0.8 A the time-delay fuse (0.8 A T) blows.

      Experiment 3 · Impedance Matching: Loudspeaker Matching

      Example 1.39: 40 V rms source with internal resistance Rs (e.g. the output of a valve amplifier, 800 Ω); the load is an 8 Ω loudspeaker. Choose the turns ratio to transfer the most power to the loudspeaker.

      Lab sheet

        Info

        An ideal transformer transfers the load to the primary as RIN = (N1/N2)² RL The power is maximum when the source sees RIN = Rs: N1/N2 = √(Rs/RL)
        Why is that? If RIN is very small the current is large but the load voltage is small; if it is very large the voltage is large but the current is small. The product (power) is maximum somewhere in between. At matching, half of the source power turns into heat in Rs: the efficiency is 50%. That is why matching is not used in the power grid, only in small-signal/audio circuits.
        Do not run an amplifier with an output transformer without a loudspeaker: when the load is removed the impedance seen from the primary becomes very large, voltage spikes appear on the primary, and the output stage/transformer insulation may be damaged. Also, at high power the loudspeaker coil heats up; increase the volume gradually.

        Experiment 4 · Dot Convention: Polarity Test

        The terminals of a transformer whose label has worn off are H1–H2 (primary) and X1–X2 (secondary). Find which secondary terminal is positive at the same time as H1 (the dot) with the voltmeter and oscilloscope methods.

        Lab sheet

          Info

          Voltmeter method: Jumper one primary terminal (H2) to one secondary terminal, apply a low AC voltage to the primary, and measure between H1 and the free secondary terminal. Vtest = V1 − V2 (subtractive) or V1 + V2 (additive) Work out yourself which case corresponds to which dot placement from the directions of the winding voltages.
          Oscilloscope method: CH1 shows H1–H2 and CH2 shows X1–X2 (the grounds of both probes are at H2 and X2; therefore H2–X2 must be common). If the waveforms are in phase, X1 has the same polarity as H1.
          Why does it matter? Polarity must be known to connect two secondaries in series to raise the voltage, to run two transformers in parallel, or to make three-phase transformer connections. A short-circuit current circulates between parallel windings connected the wrong way round.
          Do the polarity test at low voltage (10–30 V with the variac). In the additive connection the voltmeter sees V1 + V2; for a high-ratio transformer at mains voltage this value may exceed the instrument’s range.

          Experiment 5 · Circuit Solution with a Phasor Diagram

          Choose a circuit; the live phasor diagram changes together with the waveforms on the oscilloscope. In Example 1.40 design R geometrically; in 1.41–1.43 verify the diagram by measurement.

          Lab sheet

            Info

            Choice of reference: in series the common quantity is the current (I on the horizontal axis), in parallel the voltage (V on the horizontal axis). Voltage and current arrows are drawn with separate scales: only the angles can be compared.
            Idea of Example 1.40: IL and IC are in opposite directions, and their sum is a vertical arrow. For 45° between Is and IR, the two legs of the right triangle must be equal: |IR| = |IC + IL|.
            Phase on the oscilloscope: φ = 360°·Δt/T. The waveform that reaches its peak first leads. Measure Δt from the CH1 peak to the CH2 peak; if Δt < 0, CH2 leads.
            To see a current on the oscilloscope, either a current probe or a small shunt resistor (e.g. 0.1 Ω) is used. When using a shunt, the grounds of both probes must be at the same point; otherwise part of the circuit is shorted through ground. In a parallel circuit a current probe is safer for viewing the branch currents separately.
