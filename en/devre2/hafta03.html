Week 3 · Series, Parallel, Δ–Y and Kirchhoff in the Frequency Domain

Lecture notes: Chapter 1 · Part 2 — 1.5 Series, Parallel and Δ–Y Simplifications (Examples 1.18–1.25).

By the end of this lab you will

  1. measure in a series RLC circuit that the arithmetic sum of the voltmeter readings is not equal to the source, and show on a phasor diagram how KVL is satisfied with phasors.
  2. see that branch currents in a parallel circuit can be larger than the total current, and verify KCL with current phasors.
  3. find series and parallel resonance by a frequency sweep and measure element voltages that grow by a factor of Q.

Instruments on the bench

Background · Why Do Kirchhoff’s Laws Still Hold with Phasors?

The first thing that will surprise you in the lab: the sum of three voltmeters does not equal the source. This section resolves that surprise in advance.

1 · Rotating phasors and instantaneous values (Example 1.23: R = 10 Ω, L = 50 mH, C = 20 µF, Vm = 20 V)

Every sinusoid is the projection onto the real axis of an arrow rotating counter-clockwise. In a series circuit all arrows rotate at the same speed; the angles between them are constant. KVL holds at every instant: vR + vL + vC = vs. Since the projections add at every instant, the arrows themselves add tip-to-tail: VR + VL + VC = Vs. A voltmeter, however, shows only the length of the arrow. Change ω with the slider; as you approach resonance (1000 rad/s), watch the VL and VC arrows become longer than the source and cancel each other.

2 · Series, parallel, or neither?

Series: elements end to end, the current is common; impedances add and the source voltage is shared in proportion to impedance (voltage divider). Reactances add with their signs: jωL points up, −j/ωC points down.
Parallel: both terminals shared, the voltage is common; admittances add and the current is shared in proportion to admittance (current divider: the numerator is the other branch’s impedance).
Bridge: three elements meet at every node; there is no series pair and no parallel pair. The remedy is Δ–Y: convert a delta into a wye that looks the same from the terminals, revealing a series-parallel structure.

Y branch = product of the two adjacent Δ elements at that node / sum of all three Δ elements.

Worked mini example (Example 1.21): R = 30 Ω, ZC = −j40 Ω, V = 100∠0° V. Z = 30 − j40 = 50∠−53.13° Ω. VC = (40∠−90°/50∠−53.13°)·100 = 80∠−36.87° V; VR = 60∠53.13° V. The voltmeters read 60 V and 80 V: a total of 140 V! Yet in the right triangle √(60² + 80²) = 100 V ✓.

⚠ Points to watch in a real lab

Do not measure directly on the mains. These experiments are done with a function generator at a few volts. Building an RLC resonance experiment on the 230 V / 50 Hz mains can produce hundreds or even thousands of volts across the capacitor and inductor at resonance. Mains measurements are made only with an isolation transformer and under authorised supervision.
The oscilloscope ground clip is a short-circuit wire. The ground clips of the two channels are connected to each other and to mains earth inside the instrument. If you attach the clip to one end of the inductor to measure VL, you short that node to ground. For floating voltages use two channels + MATH (CH1 − CH2) or a differential probe.
At resonance the element voltage is Q times the source. With a 10 V source in a circuit with Q = 20, 200 V appears across the capacitor. Choose the capacitor’s voltage rating and the inductor’s current rating accordingly; discharge the capacitor after the experiment.
Do not use electrolytic capacitors in AC circuits. A polarised electrolytic fails during the reverse half-cycle, heats up and may explode. Choose a film (polyester/polypropylene) or bipolar capacitor.
Not every multimeter measures AC correctly. Cheap multimeters show a correct rms value only for sine waves and often only up to 400 Hz–1 kHz; those not marked “TRMS” are scaled from the average value. In the kilohertz range compare with the oscilloscope.
An ammeter is connected in series and has a fuse. Connecting an ammeter in parallel with an element short-circuits it and blows the instrument fuse. At resonance the current rises quickly: start on a high current range.
Common mistakes: adding voltmeter readings and saying “KVL failed” · adding impedances in parallel (10 Ω ∥ 10 Ω must be 5 Ω!) · putting the wrong branch in the current-divider numerator · thinking B > 0 is inductive · writing only two elements in the Δ–Y denominator (gives Z/2 instead of Z/3) · taking the generator’s set value as the terminal voltage · mixing up degrees and radians.

Experiment 1 · Series RLC: Why Don’t the Voltmeters Add Up?

Generator → R → inductor (L, rL) → C in series. Four voltmeters show the rms value of each element and of the source; the oscilloscope shows Vs on CH1 and VR (in phase with the current) on CH2.

Lab sheet

    Notes

    Why? In series the current is common. VR is in phase with the current, VL leads by 90°, VC lags by 90°. Since VL and VC point in exactly opposite directions they cancel; a voltmeter, however, shows both as positive numbers. KVL holds, but for the arrows: Vs = VR + VL + VC
    Resonance: when ωL = 1/ωC, X = 0; the current is maximum and the circuit behaves as a pure resistance. ω0 = 1/√(LC),  Q = ω0L/Rtot Here Rtot = R + rL (+ 50 Ω on the generator side). Because of rL the inductor voltmeter sees a voltage that leads by slightly less than 90°.
    Worked mini example (Example 1.18): 90 Ω, 32 mH, 5 µF, ω = 5000 rad/s: Z = 90 + j160 − j40 = 150∠53.13° Ω. For 750∠30° V, I = 5∠−23.13° A. At ω = 2500 the reactances become ±j80 and cancel: I = 750/90 = 8.33 A.
    Practical warning: to measure VL with the oscilloscope, do not attach the ground clip between the inductor and C; you would short-circuit C. That is why on a real bench element voltages are read with floating (ungrounded) TRMS voltmeters.

    Experiment 2 · Parallel RLC: Branch Currents and KCL

    R, inductor and C in parallel; each branch has an AC ammeter, the input has a total-current ammeter and a 1 Ω current-sense (shunt) resistor for the oscilloscope.

    Lab sheet

      Notes

      Why? In parallel the voltage is common. IR is in phase with the voltage, IC leads by 90°, IL lags by 90°. KCL with phasors: I = IR + IL + IC Since IL and IC are opposite they cancel in the total, but remain in the branches as a “circulating current”.
      Admittance: Y = G + jB; inductor B = −1/ωL (negative), capacitor B = +ωC (positive). The sign is opposite to that in impedance. At parallel resonance B = 0, |Y| is minimum → total current is minimum.
      Worked mini example (Example 1.20): R = 50 Ω, L = 25 mH, C = 20 µF, ω = 1000: Y = 0.02 − j0.04 + j0.02 = 0.02 − j0.02 S; at 100 V, I = 2.83∠−45° A, but |IL| = 4 A!
      Practical warning: the ammeter’s internal resistance (shunt + fuse) is small but not zero; it reduces the current in low-impedance branches. When measuring branch currents insert the ammeter in series with each branch; never connect it across two terminals.

      Experiment 3 · RC Voltage Divider, Phase Measurement and Current Divider

      Series R–C. CH1 shows the source (Vs), CH2 shows the capacitor voltage (VC). The common ground is the lower end of C, so both probes can be connected safely.

      Lab sheet

        Notes

        Voltage divider: VC/Vs = 1/(1 + jωRC) Since the ratio is complex, the divider changes both amplitude and phase. At ω = 1/RC, |VR| = |VC| = 0.707|Vs| and the phase difference is −45°.
        Two ways to measure phase: (1) With cursors, the Δt between two zero crossings in the same direction: φ = 360°·f·Δt. (2) X-Y mode: CH1 horizontal, CH2 vertical. In phase gives a line, 90° gives a circle/ellipse, in between a tilted ellipse; sin|φ| = (ellipse’s vertical-axis intercept)/(vertical peak).
        Current divider (Example 1.22): I1 = Z2/(Z1+Z2) · I The numerator is the other branch’s impedance: if Z2 grows, current shifts into the first branch.
        Practical warning: if you want to measure VR with the oscilloscope, do not attach the ground clip between R and C; C would be short-circuited. Use CH1 − CH2 (MATH) instead, or swap R and C.

          Notes

          Why Δ–Y? In the bridge three elements meet at every node; there is no series pair because current splits into a third branch, and no parallel pair because the terminals differ. Converting Δ to Y reveals the a–b–n and a–c–n branches; these are in parallel between a and n.
          Worked mini example: Σ = 10 + (20 + j60) + (−j20) = 30 + j40. Z1 = 10(20 + j60)/Σ = 12 + j4 Ω. Then Zabn = −j4 + 12 + j4 = 12 Ω (the reactances cancelled!). Find the remaining two branches yourself.
          Practical warning: the LCR meter generates its own test signal; measuring while another source is connected to the circuit gives a wrong result and may damage the instrument’s input. Disconnect the source, discharge large capacitors, then measure.
