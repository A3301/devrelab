Background · Why Three Phases, and How Do They Work?

Set aside 15 minutes: rotating phasors, the two connection types, the formula card and the hazards waiting for you on the mains.

1 · Rotating phasors and three sine waves

The three windings are placed 120° apart on the generator shaft. As the magnet rotates, a sine wave of the same amplitude is induced in each winding, but each peaks 120° (T/3 = 6.67 ms at 50 Hz) after the previous one. In the animation each phasor rotates at constant speed; the wave is the horizontal (Re) projection of the phasor. In the abc sequence the peaks come in the order a → b → c; if you swap two cables it becomes acb and a three-phase motor runs in reverse.

Why is that? The sum of three equal phasors 120° apart is zero: va + vb + vc = 0 at every instant. This is why no current flows in the neutral conductor under a balanced load, and why the same power can be carried with less copper than single-phase (Example 3.1). Tick “Show instantaneous sum” and check.

2 · Phase voltage, line voltage

Phase voltage is between a phase terminal and neutral (Van). Line voltage is between two phase terminals (Vab). The difference of two phasors of the same amplitude but 120° apart is √3 times larger and leads by 30° in abc. Warning: subtracting amplitudes (230 − 230 = 0) is wrong; the phasors are subtracted.

Solved mini example (Example 3.12): 400 V line, abc, line 0.5 + j1 Ω, load 7.5 + j5 Ω. ① Vφ = 400/√3 = 230.9 V. ② Ztotal = 8 + j6 = 10∠36.87° Ω. ③ IaA = 230.9/10 = 23.09∠−36.87° A; IbB and IcC have the same amplitude, shifted by −120° and +120°. ④ Load phase voltage |VAN| = 23.09·|7.5+j5| = 208.2 V; a 9.9% drop on the line.

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

400 V kills. Phase-to-phase is 400 V and phase-to-neutral 230 V; the peak reaches 565 V. Always build the circuit de-energised, apply lockout–tagout, work with one hand, and use insulated (CAT III) probes and leads. The bench must have a residual-current device (30 mA).
The oscilloscope ground clip is never connected to the mains. The ground lead of an ordinary probe is tied to protective earth; clipping it to a phase terminal means a direct short circuit and an explosion. For phase–neutral and phase–phase measurements use a differential probe or an isolation transformer. Never defeat the oscilloscope’s earthing pin.
The neutral must never break. If the neutral breaks under an unbalanced load, the star point shifts; appliances on the lightly loaded phase can burn out at voltages above 300 V (Experiment 4). The neutral is not fused or switched on its own; its connections must be tight and its cross-section sufficient.
Do not run a motor without checking the phase sequence. A pump, fan or lift running in reverse causes damage. Before connecting, verify abc with a phase-sequence indicator; swapping two phases reverses the sequence.
Instrument category and range. Use the multimeter on the V~ range with the correct sockets; measuring voltage with a probe in the current socket blows the fuse or the meter. A clamp ammeter must enclose one conductor alone; enclosing two conductors together makes the currents cancel.
The lamps in a phase-sequence indicator see up to 1.5 times Vφ because the star point shifts. Therefore either two lamps in series or a 400 V-rated indicator is used; the capacitor must be rated at least 400 V AC and discharged when finished.
Common mistakes: writing +120° for phase b in abc (correct: −120°) · taking the 400 V on the label as the phase voltage (forgetting √3) · finding the line voltage zero by subtracting amplitudes · using +30° in acb · assuming the load voltage equals the source voltage (forgetting the line drop) · solving only phase a for an unbalanced load and shifting b, c by ±120°.

The three phase–neutral voltages on the same screen via differential probes. Source A is labelled (abc); the sequence of Source X is unknown.

Lab sheet

    Info

    The oscilloscope triggers on the rising zero crossing of CH1 (T▼ mark on screen). The CURSOR button opens two vertical cursors; Δt = tB − tA is read directly. Phase difference: θ = 360°·Δt / T
    Phase-sequence indicator: a capacitor is connected to phase a and a lamp to each of phases b and c; all three join at a common floating star point. Because the load is unbalanced the star point shifts and the two lamps glow with different brightness. Which one is bright depends on the sequence.
    Why is that? The capacitor current leads its voltage by 90°; the star point is therefore pushed away from the phase “following” phase a. When the sequence changes, the following phase changes too and the bright lamp swaps places.
    On a real bench, do not clip the ground lead of an ordinary oscilloscope probe even to neutral: there may be a few volts and leakage current between neutral and protective earth. Do not forget to set the differential probe ratio (e.g. 1:100) on the oscilloscope.

    Experiment 2 · Phase and Line Voltage: √3 and 30°

    Connect the two multimeter leads and the two oscilloscope channels between any terminals you like. The phasor panel draws the difference phasor (orange) measured by the DMM.

    Lab sheet

      Info

      The line voltage is the difference of two phase voltages: Vab = Van − Vbn = √3 Van∠±30° The plus sign is for abc, minus for acb.
      Why √3? If the angle between two equal phasors is 120°, the length of their difference is 2·Vφ·sin60° = √3·Vφ. On the phasor panel, see that the orange arrow joins the tips of a–n and b–n.
      Mini example: at 50 Hz, 30° = T/12 = 1.667 ms. In abc the peak of vab comes, relative to the peak of van, 1.667 ms earlier (leading).
      When connecting the DMM between phases, make sure it is rated 750 V AC and CAT III. Connect the probes to COM first, then to the red terminal; reverse the order when disconnecting.

      Experiment 3 · Balanced Y–Y Circuit, Neutral Current and Single-Phase Equivalent

      Generator internal impedance, line impedance and a Y load. The lab counterpart of Example 3.11 (Nilsson Example 11.1).

      Lab sheet

        Info

        Single-phase equivalent circuit: in a balanced Y–Y the neutrals are at the same potential, so we may imagine a lossless wire between them. Phase a is solved on its own: IaA = Va′n / (Zg + Zℓ + ZL) Phases b and c have the same amplitude, shifted by −120° and +120°.
        Why is the neutral current zero? I0 = IaA + IbB + IcC; the sum of three phasors of equal amplitude 120° apart is zero. Imagine the dashed current arrows on the phasor panel placed tip to tail: they form a closed triangle.
        Solution path (Example 3.11): ① Add the series impedances: (0.2+0.8+39) + j(0.5+1.5+28). ② Convert to polar. ③ Divide 120∠0° V by it. ④ VAN = ZL·IaA, VAB = √3 VAN∠30°. Compare the results with measurement.
        Close the clamp ammeter around one conductor only. When measuring the neutral current, enclose the neutral conductor separately. With current-transformer measurement, never leave the secondary open: a high voltage develops.

        Experiment 4 · Unbalanced Load and the Broken-Neutral Hazard

        Three separate resistive (lamp) loads. Open the neutral switch to simulate a broken neutral in a household installation. The brightness of the lamps shows the voltage across them.

        Lab sheet

          Info

          With the neutral broken (Z0 → ∞) the load star point N′ shifts away from the source neutral: VN′n = (Va/ZA + Vb/ZB + Vc/ZC) / (1/ZA + 1/ZB + 1/ZC) The load phase voltages are now measured with respect to N′: VAN′ = Va − VN′n.
          Why does the lightly loaded phase see an overvoltage? N′ is pulled towards the voltage of the phase with the smallest impedance (the most heavily loaded one). That phase’s voltage drops, and the voltages of the other two phases rise. Watch the red N′ point on the phasor panel.
          Solution (Example 3.9): YA = YB = 0.05 S, YC = 0.1 S. Numerator = 120(0.05 + 0.05∠−120° + 0.1∠120°) = 6∠120°; denominator 0.2 ⇒ VN = 30∠120° V.
          In a real installation a broken neutral causes appliances such as refrigerators and televisions to burn out, and fires. Neutral connections must be tightened periodically, and the neutral cross-section must not be chosen smaller than the phase cross-section. Perform this experiment only with a low-voltage training set.
