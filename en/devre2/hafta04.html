Background · Reducing a Circuit to Two Numbers

The mains socket, an audio amplifier output, a sensor: seen from their terminals, we see each of them as “a source + an impedance”. This week we will do this by measurement.

1 · Why an equivalent circuit?

We do not want to re-solve the whole network every time we change the load. A linear, single-frequency network, seen from terminals a–b, behaves either as VTh + series ZTh (Thevenin) or as IN + parallel ZN (Norton). The bridge between them is source transformation: VTh = ZTh·IN, ZN = ZTh.

2 · Three ways to find ZTh

(1) Zero the independent sources (V → short, I → open) and look into a–b. (2) Ratio: ZTh = Voc/Isc — we will use this in the lab because we cannot open the box; but in AC this is a phasor division: magnitudes divide, angles subtract. (3) With dependent sources, a test source: ZTh = VT/IT.

Worked mini example (Example 1.28): 120∠0° V, series 8 + j6 Ω, 12 − j16 Ω across a–b. Short circuit: IN = 120/(8 + j6) = 12∠−36.87° A. Source shorted: ZN = (8 + j6) ∥ (12 − j16) = 8.8 + j1.6 Ω. VTh = INZN = 96 − j48 V. With a 10 Ω load IL = 5.69∠−31.43° A.

3 · What happens when the load changes? (interactive)

VTh = 10∠0° V, ZTh = 50 + jX Ω. As the purely resistive RL goes from 0 to ∞, the tip of the VL phasor travels along the dashed orange arc: zero at RL = 0, VTh at RL = ∞. When X ≠ 0 the load voltage is phase-shifted relative to VTh.

⚠ Points to watch in a real lab

Never measure the “short-circuit current” of the mains. The Thevenin impedance of a 230 V socket is on the order of fractions of an ohm; the short-circuit current is hundreds to thousands of amperes and means arcs, burns and fire. The mains equivalent can be found only from the voltage drop under load, with a suitable instrument and under authorised supervision.
Make short-circuit measurements only on current-limited, low-power sources. The black box or generator must withstand a short circuit. Short-circuiting a transformer secondary heats the winding; the secondary of a current transformer, on the contrary, must never be left open (a high voltage develops).
Range and fuse when measuring short-circuit current with an ammeter. Start on the highest current range (10 A input), then go lower. If the fuse in the mA input blows, the instrument shows “0” and you think the box delivers no current.
Oscilloscope ground and REF. Terminal b of the black box, the REF output ground and the oscilloscope ground are the same point. Attaching the ground clip to terminal a short-circuits the box; that is why the shunt resistor is placed on the b (ground) side.
The instrument is also a load. A 10 MΩ voltmeter is negligible in most circuits, but a 1 MΩ oscilloscope input and its probe (~100 pF with a ×1 probe) affect high-impedance sources and high frequencies; the ammeter’s internal resistance makes the short circuit less than “perfect”.
Discharge with capacitive loads. Before changing a series C load, disconnect the source and discharge the capacitor through a resistor; energy may remain, especially in large capacitances.
Common mistakes: saying ZTh = |Voc|/|Isc| and forgetting the angle · adding angles in division · measuring Voc and Isc against references with different phases · mixing up Vpp, peak and rms values · zeroing a dependent source · taking the amplitude on the generator display as the open-circuit voltage · including the element of interest in the source transformation.

Experiment 1 · Thevenin Equivalent of the Black Box (as Phasors)

The box has two terminals (a, b) and a REF output synchronous with the internal source. The oscilloscope shows REF on CH1 and the probe you select on CH2: the a–b voltage or the voltage across the 1 Ω shunt (= current).

Lab sheet

    Notes

    Why are two measurements enough? The a–b behaviour of a linear network is like a straight line (in the phasor plane): V = VTh − ZThI. Two points (I = 0 and V = 0) determine the line: Voc = VTh, Isc = VTh/ZTh.
    Why phase? Magnitudes alone give |ZTh|; but separating RTh and XTh requires the angle: θ = ∠VTh − ∠IN. Since the two measurements are made at different times, a common reference (REF) is essential.
    Worked mini example: suppose Voc = 2.40 V ∠−20° and Isc = 30.0 mA ∠+25° are measured. |Z| = 2.40/0.030 = 80 Ω, θ = −20° − 25° = −45° → ZTh = 56.6 − j56.6 Ω (capacitive).
    Practical warning: the short circuit is not “really” zero ohms: the ammeter (0.1 Ω) and shunt (1 Ω) are in series. When |ZTh| is a few tens of ohms this means a 2–4% error; discuss it in the report. For low-impedance sources, measurements with two different loads are used instead of a short-circuit measurement.

      Prediction: |VL| = |VTh·Zx/(ZTh + Zx)|, Zx = ZL + 1.1 Ω (ammeter + shunt). Error = (model − measurement)/measurement.

      Notes

      Why try a reactive load too? Purely resistive loads are sensitive only to |ZTh| and partly to the angle. An R + C load cancels part of XTh (similar to series resonance) and the current rises noticeably: if you got the sign of XTh wrong, the error grows here.
      Power hint: with a purely resistive load, the load power is maximum around RL = |ZTh|. (The exact condition for maximum power transfer, ZL = ZTh*, is the topic of later weeks.)
      Practical warning: check the power rating of the load resistor: P = I²R. With low-resistance loads the current grows; a ¼ W resistor heats up and changes value, and your measurement drifts.

      Experiment 3 · The Function Generator’s 50 Ω, Source Transformation and Norton

      The generator you use every day is not an ideal source: it contains a 50 Ω series resistance. Its display shows the amplitude based on a “50 Ω” or “High-Z” load assumption.

      Lab sheet

        Model panel

        VTh (Vpp)RTh (Ω)
        IN (mA, peak)RN (Ω)
        When you select a model, the bench generator is removed from the circuit and your model drives the load. If it gives the same voltage as the real generator at the same RL, your model is equivalent at the terminals.

        Notes

        Why is the display “wrong”? The generator output is designed to be connected to a 50 Ω coaxial line and a 50 Ω termination. In “50 Ω” mode the display shows E/2 (the voltage across a 50 Ω load). When you connect it to the oscilloscope’s 1 MΩ input you measure E, i.e. twice as much.
        Source transformation: E and series 50 Ω ↔ IN = E/50 Ω and parallel 50 Ω. Going from Vpp to peak current: IN,peak = (VTh,pp/2)/RTh.
        Practical warning: short-circuiting the generator output does no harm on most instruments (the 50 Ω limits the current) but heats the output stage; do not leave it for long. Never connect an external DC voltage (e.g. a supply) to the generator output.
