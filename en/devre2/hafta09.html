Background · From Heating Equivalent to Power Triangle

Spend 15 minutes before starting the experiments: two live figures, a formula card and the pitfalls of a real lab.

1 · Why the “effective” (rms) value?

How do we compare an AC source whose voltage changes every instant with DC? The idea is simple: the DC voltage that produces the same average heat in the same resistor is the effective value of the AC. Since heat is proportional to v²/R, we first square, then average over a period, then take the square root: Root–Mean–Square.

Vrms = √( (1/T) ∫ v² dt ),   for a sine Vrms = Vm/√2 ≈ 0.707 Vm

In the figure below the blue curve is v(t) and the orange curve is v²(t). The dashed line is the mean of v², and the green line is its square root, Vrms. The moving cursor accumulates the mean step by step. Change the waveform and see where the green line sits relative to the peak.

2 · Complex power and the power triangle

In an inductive or capacitive load there is an angle θ = θv − θi between current and voltage. The instantaneous power p(t) is a wave with mean P that oscillates at 2ω. Part of the oscillating component is energy going back and forth between source and load: its amplitude is the reactive power Q. We combine both into one complex number:

S = P + jQ = Vrms Irms* = |S|∠θ,   pf = cos θ = P/|S|

Change P and Q with the sliders; the legs of the triangle are P (horizontal, doing work) and Q (vertical, going back and forth), and the hypotenuse is |S|. If Q > 0 the current lags (lagging pf, inductive); if Q < 0 it leads (leading pf, capacitive).

Why does an average-responding meter go wrong?

Cheap multimeters do not really compute rms: they rectify the signal, take its average, then multiply by the form factor valid for a sine, π/(2√2) ≈ 1.111. This is correct for a sine but wrong for other waveforms. For a square wave the average and rms are equal (FF = 1), but the meter still multiplies by 1.111, so it reads 11% high. For a triangle FF = 1.155, so it reads 3.8% low.

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

Mains voltage kills. The peak value of 230 V is 325 V. In mains-connected experiments use only insulated (CAT II/III rated) probes and shrouded banana leads; never change connections while energised, work with one hand, and do not wear rings or watches.
Isolation transformer and variac. Always run load experiments behind the isolation transformer and start with the variac at zero. A variac alone does not provide isolation: one output terminal may be connected directly to the mains phase.
The oscilloscope ground clip must not touch the mains. The scope’s ground clip is tied to the building’s protective earth. If it is attached to a point on the mains side, a phase–earth short circuit results: the probe burns, the fuse blows, and the arc can injure you. Use a differential probe for mains measurements.
Measure current in series or with a clamp. Connecting the leads across a voltage while the multimeter is on a current range blows the meter’s fuse (or the meter itself). For currents above 10 A use a current clamp or a current transformer; never leave the secondary of a current transformer open while it carries load current.
Know your meter’s limits. TRMS meters have a crest factor limit (typically CF ≤ 3) and a bandwidth (typically 1–100 kHz). With narrow pulses or at high frequency, even a true rms meter makes errors.
Power rating of the load resistor. Applying 10 V rms to 100 Ω gives 1 W: a ¼ W resistor will burn. If you compute with the peak value you get the power wrong by a factor of 2; choose resistors using the rms value.
Discharge capacitive loads. Large capacitors in the AC load box (hundreds of µF) can stay charged at peak voltage (≈325 V) after power is removed. Discharge them through a discharge resistor before touching.
Common mistakes: writing P = Vm²/R with the peak value (2 times too high) · thinking “the average of a sine is zero, so its rms is zero too” · writing S = V I and forgetting the conjugate (the sign of Q comes out reversed) · thinking “lagging pf” is capacitive (lagging = current lags = inductive, Q > 0) · adding the |S| values of two loads directly · writing S = V I* with peak phasors and forgetting the ½.

Experiment 1 · Waveform, rms Value and Two Multimeters

A function generator drives a load resistor. Two multimeters measure the same voltage: one is true rms (TRMS), the other is average-responding. On the oscilloscope CH1 = v(t), CH2 = MATH channel p(t) = v²/R.

Lab sheet

    Information

    The definition is the same for every waveform: Vrms = √( (1/T)∫v² dt ) Only the result changes: a square wave is at ±Vm at every instant, so Vrms = Vm; a triangle spends most of its time away from the peak, so Vm/√3.
    On a TRMS meter, AC coupling removes the DC component (capacitor coupling); AC+DC gives the total rms: Vrms = √(VDC² + Vac,rms²)
    Why is that? Heating power depends on the mean of v², not on the mean of |v|. An average-responding meter measures the mean of |v| and multiplies by 1.111 assuming a sine; if the wave is not a sine, this factor is wrong. The meter does not “lie”; it makes a wrong assumption.
    Worked mini example (Example 2.11): i(t) = 4cos(ωt+30°) A flows through R = 25 Ω. Irms = 4/√2 = 2.828 A; P = Irms²R = 8 × 25 = 200 W; equivalent DC voltage = IrmsR = 70.71 V. The phase angle (30°) does not affect the power in the resistor.
    The function generator has a 50 Ω internal resistance. If you choose a very small load resistor (e.g. 10 Ω) the generator output collapses and the voltage on the load differs from the setting on the display. Always measure across the load.

    Experiment 2 · Complex Power, Power Triangle and Load Design

    A variac with an isolation transformer supplies a series R–L–C load box. The power analyser sits between source and load and shows V, I, P, Q, |S| and pf.

    Lab sheet

      Information

      Series load: Z = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC)),  S = Vrms²/Z* = Irms² Z The sign of Q is the sign of the imaginary part of Z: if the inductive part dominates, Q > 0.
      Instantaneous power (voltage as reference, θv = 0): p(t) = P + P cos 2ωt + Q sin 2ωt (The form p = P + P cos 2ωt − Q sin 2ωt in the lecture notes holds when time zero is taken at the current peak; the two expressions differ only by a shift of the time axis.) Its mean is P; Q is the amplitude of the energy going back and forth between source and load. When p(t) goes negative in the plot, the load is giving energy back to the source.
      Why S = V I*? If V = |V|∠θv and I = |I|∠θi, then V I* = |V||I|∠(θv−θi). The conjugate turns the angle into a difference, so the real part is P = |V||I|cos θ and the imaginary part is Q = |V||I|sin θ. If you forget the conjugate, the angle becomes θv+θi, which is meaningless.
      Worked mini example (Example 2.12): 240 V, P = 8 kW, pf = 0.8 lagging. |S| = 8/0.8 = 10 kVA, Q = +√(10²−8²) = +6 kVAR → S = 8 + j6 kVA. Impedance: Z = V²/S* = 240²/(8000 − j6000) = 5.76∠36.87° Ω. Find the real and imaginary parts and L yourself, and build it.
      In this experiment the current can reach 40 A. In reality, for currents at this level the cable cross-section (≥ 6 mm²), terminal tightness and the power analyser’s current input or clamp must be chosen appropriately. A loose terminal heats up and can start a fire.

      Experiment 3 · Parallel Loads: How Do Powers Add?

      Two loads are connected to a 240 V bus through switches K1 and K2. You can place the power analyser’s current clamp on the source line, on Load 1 or on Load 2.

      Lab sheet

        Information

        Complex power is conserved (Tellegen): ST = S1 + S2 → PT = P1+P2, QT = Q1+Q2 But in general |ST| ≤ |S1| + |S2|
        Each load is modelled as a fixed impedance derived from the given P and pf at 240 V. If you change the bus voltage, the powers change with V².
        Why does |S| not add? S is a vector. The Q of an inductive load (+) and the Q of a capacitive load (−) cancel each other; the total triangle gets “flatter”. The arithmetic sum of apparent powers is correct only if both loads have the same pf.
        Worked mini example: Load A: 6 kW, pf 0.6 lagging → |S| = 10 kVA, Q = +8 kVAR. Load B: 6 kW, pf 1 → Q = 0. Total: 12 + j8 kVA, |ST| = 14.42 kVA (not 16); pfT = 12/14.42 = 0.832 lagging.
        When attaching the current clamp make sure its jaws are fully closed and that only one conductor (phase or neutral) passes through it. If you clamp phase and neutral together, the currents cancel and the clamp reads zero.
