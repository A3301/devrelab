Background · From Sinusoid to Phasor

Why always sinusoids? Because the mains, generators and the steady state of every linear circuit are sinusoidal; the derivative, integral and sum of a sinusoid are again sinusoids of the same frequency.

1 · Three parameters

v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φ),  ω = 2πf = 2π/T

Vm is the peak value (the curve swings between −Vm and +Vm), ω is the angle swept per second (rad/s), and φ is the position at t = 0. If φ > 0, the peak comes before t = 0: the curve leads. Leading does not mean being larger; only the timing of the peaks matters.

2 · Why rms?

The average of a sinusoid is zero, yet it heats a resistor. Heat is proportional to the average of v²; since the average of v² is Vm²/2, the DC voltage giving the same heat is Vrms = Vm/√2. The “230 V” at the wall socket is rms: its peak value is 325 V. Insulation is chosen by the peak value, heating by the rms value.

3 · Rotating phasor: the “hidden” circle of a sinusoid

Euler: ejθ = cos θ + j sin θ. Hence v = Re{Vmejφ · ejωt} The number Vmejφ = Vm∠φ is the phasor: it carries amplitude and phase, not frequency. ejωt rotates it counterclockwise at speed ω; the projection of its tip on the real axis is v(t). Watch this in the animation on the right.

What should you see? When the phasor reaches the real axis (ωt = −φ) the projection is largest: v peaks. One revolution is one period. Since both phasors rotate at the same speed, the angle between them never changes: their sum is also a single phasor rotating at the same speed. This is the essence of why phasor addition works.

Worked mini example (Example 1.8)

y = 20cos(ωt − 30°) + 40cos(ωt + 60°) = ?
1) Phasors: 20∠−30° = 17.32 − j10; 40∠60° = 20 + j34.64.
2) Add (in rectangular form!): 37.32 + j24.64.
3) Convert to polar: √(37.32² + 24.64²) = 44.72; tan−1(24.64/37.32) = 33.43°.
4) y = 44.72 cos(ωt + 33.43°). The amplitude is not 60, because the two waves do not peak at the same time.

⚠ Things to watch in a real lab

Do not measure the mains directly with an oscilloscope. The ground clip of the oscilloscope probe is connected to the protective earth of the mains. Attaching the clip to the line or neutral conductor creates a short circuit: the probe, the oscilloscope and you can be harmed. Mains measurements are made only with a differential probe or behind an isolation transformer. “Floating” the oscilloscope’s ground pin (unearthed plug) is strictly forbidden.
230 V rms = 325 V peak. Insulation, probe and capacitor voltage ratings are chosen by the peak value. Use a 10:1 probe and check the probe’s maximum voltage rating (CAT class).
Both ground clips to the same point. In a two-channel oscilloscope the grounds of the two probes are connected internally. Attaching the CH1 and CH2 ground clips to different nodes of the circuit shorts the element between them; in phase-difference measurements both signals must be taken with respect to a common ground.
A multimeter does not measure rms for every waveform. Low-cost meters measure the average value and multiply by 1.11 assuming a sine. They read incorrectly on triangle or square waves; use meters marked “True RMS”. The bandwidth of an AC voltmeter is also limited (most are accurate up to about 1 kHz).
Back EMF when switching an inductor. Opening the circuit of a current-carrying inductor produces a very high voltage L·di/dt; sparks appear at the switch contacts. In Experiment 4, turn off the source before opening the circuit or provide a freewheeling path (resistor/diode).
The internal resistance of the function generator is 50 Ω. With a low-impedance load the output voltage is smaller than the displayed value; always verify the measured amplitude on the oscilloscope. Do not short the output.
Common mistakes: taking 230 V as the peak and writing P = 230²/R · adding φ in degrees while ωt is in radians (not cos(1 + 30) but cos(57.3° + 30°)) · writing a phasor without converting sin to cos (10 sin(ωt+30°) ↔ 10∠−60°) · adding the amplitudes of two sinusoids directly · treating the inductor as a short circuit in steady state and getting I = Vm/R · confusing ω and f (the 2π factor).

Experiment 1 · Parameters of a Sinusoid and the rms Value

Connect the function generator to CH1 of the oscilloscope and, at the same time, to a true-rms voltmeter and a low-cost (average-responding) multimeter.

Lab sheet

    Information

    On the oscilloscope the period is the time between two consecutive peaks (or two zero crossings in the same direction): f = 1/T, ω = 2πf. Put cursor A on one peak and B on the next; Δt = T.
    Vpp = 2Vm (a sinusoid is symmetric). The oscilloscope shows peak values, the voltmeter shows rms. Example 1.3: 34 Vpp on screen ⇒ Vm = 17 V ⇒ Vrms = 12.02 V.
    Why 1/√3 for a triangle? For a sine, v² has the cos² form whose average is Vm²/2; for a triangle it consists of t² parabolas whose average is Vp²/3 (Example 1.1). A square wave is always at ±Vp, so rms = Vp.
    The reading of a low-cost multimeter is correct only for a sine. When reporting AC measurements in a lab report, state whether the meter is “True RMS” and give its frequency range.

    Experiment 2 · Phase Difference Between Two Sinusoids: Δt and Lissajous

    The two outputs of the generator (or the input/output of the black box) are connected to CH1 and CH2. You will measure the phase difference in two ways.

    Lab sheet

      Information

      Time method: Measure Δt between same-direction zero crossings (or peaks) of the two curves: Δφ = 360° · Δt / T If the peak of CH2 comes after that of CH1, CH2 lags (Δφ negative).
      Lissajous method: In X-Y mode the horizontal axis is CH1 and the vertical axis is CH2. Two sinusoids of the same frequency trace an ellipse. If the vertical intercept of the ellipse at x = 0 is Y0 and its largest vertical extent is Ym, then |sin Δφ| = Y0/Ym Δφ = 0 gives a straight line (/), 90° a circle or an axis-aligned ellipse, 180° a reversed line (\).
      Why sin? x = A cos ωt, y = B cos(ωt + Δφ). When x = 0, ωt = 90° and y = B cos(90° + Δφ) = −B sin Δφ. So the intercept is B|sin Δφ|. Lissajous does not tell the sign: whether it leads or lags is found from the direction of rotation of the ellipse or from the Δt method.
      In X-Y mode, if the V/div settings of the two channels differ, the ellipse looks skewed, but the ratio Y0/Ym does not change since it is read only from the vertical channel. Select the same AC/DC coupling for both channels; a DC offset shifts the ellipse.

      Experiment 3 · Adding Sinusoids with Phasors

      Two sinusoidal sources are connected in series; the total voltage across them is v = v1 + v2. CH1 shows the reference cos ωt (the generator’s SYNC output), CH2 shows the signal you select.

      Lab sheet

        Information

        The sum of two sinusoids of the same frequency is again a sinusoid of that frequency. Its amplitude and phase are found by adding the phasors like vectors: V = V1 + V2 = (a1+a2) + j(b1+b2)
        To measure the phase of the sum on the oscilloscope, measure the shift of the CH2 peak relative to the CH1 (cos ωt, phase 0) peak: φ = −360°·Δt/T (φ is negative if the CH2 peak comes later).
        Why don’t the amplitudes add? 2 + 4 = 6 only if the two waves peak at the same time (same phase). If they are 90° apart the sum is √(2²+4²) = 4.47; if 180°, |4 − 2| = 2. The parallelogram in the phasor diagram shows this geometrically.
        In practice, to connect two generators in series, the output of at least one must be isolated (floating) from ground; otherwise the ground of the second generator shorts the output of the first. With single-chassis two-channel generators, the addition is done with the oscilloscope’s MATH (CH1+CH2) function.

        Experiment 4 · Transient and Sinusoidal Steady State in an RL Circuit

        At t = 0 the switch closes and vs = Vmcos(ωt + φ) is applied to the series RL circuit. The oscilloscope is in single-shot (SINGLE) mode, with the trigger locked to the switch closing instant; CH2 is a current probe (1 V/A).

        Lab sheet

          Information

          Complete response = decaying transient + persistent steady state: i = K e−t/τ + Im cos(ωt + φ − θ) K = i(0) − Imcos(φ − θ). Since the inductor current cannot jump, i(0) = 0.
          5τ rule: after 5τ the transient component falls to 0.7% of its initial value. Only R and L enter τ; the source’s ω does not: the decay is a property of the circuit itself.
          Why is there no transient at some angles? If the steady-state curve already passes through zero at t = 0 (φ − θ = ±90°), the condition i(0) = 0 is satisfied by itself and K = 0. The largest transient occurs when φ = θ or θ + 180°: this is why the “inrush current” of transformers and motors depends on the switching instant.
          In a real circuit do not open the switch while the inductor carries current: L di/dt produces sparks and overvoltage. At the end of the experiment turn off the source first. You are working with 100 V peak: do not touch the circuit while it is energized, and attach the current probe while it is de-energized.
